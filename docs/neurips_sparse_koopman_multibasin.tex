\begin{abstract}
Koopman autoencoders learn coordinates in which nonlinear dynamics are advanced by a linear latent transition. Multibasin systems make this goal structurally difficult: local linearizations, and sometimes linearizations valid across individual basins, can exist around individual attracting sets, but different basins generally require incompatible finite-dimensional, state-reconstructing linear descriptions. We study sparse-latent Koopman autoencoders, where the encoder is biased to activate only a small subset of latent coordinates. The central hypothesis is that this active support can make the relevant basin or local linear law inspectable for the current state. We test this hypothesis by separating forecasting from interpretability, asking whether supports agree with basin labels in controlled benchmarks, whether they can route local predictors without oracle basin labels, and whether sparse-latent models remain competitive at long horizons. The evidence supports a qualified view. On states far from basin boundaries, sparse representations often form stable supports whose members come from a single basin, suggesting that the encoder recovers part of the attractor organization. However, exact support identity is not a reliable global invariant; it is sensitive to thresholds, basin boundaries, and equivalent latent coordinate choices. For deployment, fixed-size support routing is more robust than thresholded exact matching, and support-conditioned local predictors often improve on applying one global latent transition everywhere. Long-horizon forecasting results show that sparse-latent models remain competitive beyond controlled multibasin benchmarks, with stronger transition structure becoming most useful at the longest horizons. The main conclusion is therefore not that each basin has one exact support, but that induced latent sparsity can reveal support families that help identify the relevant basin or local predictor.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Learning accurate and interpretable models of nonlinear dynamical systems remains a central problem in machine learning, scientific computing, and control. One particularly attractive direction is to seek a representation in which the nonlinear dynamics can become locally linear, since linear models support efficient prediction, estimation, and control while remaining amenable to classical solving tools such as linear quadratic regulators (LQR) \citep{kalman_general_1960} and model predictive control (MPC) \citep{mayne_constrained_2000, grune_nonlinear_2017}. Classical linearization theory gives local topological conjugacies near hyperbolic equilibria \citep{grobman1959homeomorphism,hartman1960lemma}, and Koopman eigenfunction theory extends related linearizing coordinates to entire basins of attraction for stable equilibria and periodic orbits \citep{lan_linearization_2013,kvalheim_existence_2021}. So indeed, a bridge between nonlinear and linear dynamics can be found theoretically.

Koopman operator theory provides a natural way to do this. Instead of evolving the state in the nonlinear dynamical system directly, Koopman theory studies the evolution of observables under a linear operator acting on a lifted function space \citep{koopman_hamiltonian_1931, koopman_dynamical_1932, mezic2005spectral, brunton_modern_2022}. This viewpoint has inspired a large line of literature on data-driven approximations of Koopman dynamics, such as DMD and eDMD \citep{schmid_dmd_2010, williams_datadriven_2015, williams_kernel-based_2016}, and applications of these linear predictors for nonlinear control via MPC \citep{korda_linear_2018} or LQR \citep{mamakoukas_local_2019}.
More recently, Koopman autoencoders which learn this lift from data have emerged from integrating deep learning with Koopman theory \citep{takeishi2017learning,lusch_deep_2018,otto2019linearly, azencot_forecasting_2020, shi_deep_2022, fathi2024course}. Koopman autoencoders learn an encoder which maps a state to a latent representation, a matrix that advances that representation linearly, and a decoder that maps the latent state back to the original state space.

A central challenge of Koopman approximations is that the most interesting nonlinear systems typically contain multiple equilibria or multiple basins of attraction, and theory shows that these systems cannot admit a single finite-dimensional global linearization \citep{lan_linearization_2013, brunton_koopman_2016, pan_lifting_2024}.
For example, a damped mechanical system can settle into different wells; a biological or chemical model can approach different stable equilibria; a controlled system can move between regions with distinct local dynamics.
In such multistable systems, it is possible to linearize the dynamics within basins, but the linearizations needed in different basins of attraction are incompatible with each other.
% The problem goes beyond predicting trajectories within a single locally valid basin to representing dynamics that may move across qualitatively different regimes while preserving enough structure to support downstream reasoning and control.
Given the theoretical argument, multibasin forecasting with a single global finite-dimensional linearization seems ill-posed; it is more naturally a problem of discovering which local linear description, or which description valid across a basin, applies at the current state.


If Koopman models are to serve as a foundation for interpretable multi-regime modeling, then the latent representation of a Koopman autoencoder should do more than forecast well: ideally, it should organize the state space into meaningful local dynamical regimes. Our hypothesis is that sparse coding offers an appealing inductive bias for this purpose. Classical sparse coding and LISTA-style encoders produce piecewise-linear mappings and union-of-subspaces structure in latent space \citep{olshausen_emergence_1996, chen_atomic_1998, donoho_optimally_2003, gregor_learning_2010}.
If the latent representation of state produced by a LISTA encoder is sparse, then each state activates only a small subset of latent coordinates. That active subset, which we call a support, is a discrete object that can be inspected and used for routing to correct locally linear dynamics. Then, periodically decoding and re-encoding the state can refresh the quality of the latent embedding over time by re-selecting the correct local dynamics as trajectories move across the state space \citep{fathi2024course}.

This paper tests that hypothesis in a deliberately label-free learning setting. During training and deployment, the model is not told the number of basins, the basin assignment of any trajectory, or which local linear law should be used. Basin labels, when available in controlled benchmarks, are reserved for evaluation. The contribution is an empirical and mechanistic study of whether induced latent sparsity produces support objects that agree with basin labels in evaluation, whether those support objects can route local predictors without oracle labels, and whether the resulting sparse-latent Koopman models remain competitive on long-horizon forecasting tasks.

The paper follows this chain directly. \Cref{sec:problem} formulates multibasin Koopman learning, \cref{sec:sparse-coding} explains why sparse supports can identify local dynamical regimes, and \cref{sec:method} defines the sparse-latent model, support objects, re-encoding rule, and training objective. \Cref{sec:experiments} then states the evaluation protocol, including the support-routed prediction diagnostics, and \cref{sec:results} presents the evidence in the same order as the claim: agreement between supports and basin labels, non-oracle support-routed prediction, support refresh after transfer into a new basin, and long-horizon forecasting.

\section{Problem formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

The introduction motivates a multibasin version of Koopman learning. Given sampled trajectories of a nonlinear system, the aim is to learn a state-reconstructing latent representation whose evolution is linear, without being told which basin or local dynamical regime generated each observation. The benchmarks may be defined by continuous-time dynamics, but the learner is only given stored observations. Thus, for each system, we work with the one-step observation map
\begin{equation}
  x_{k+1}=F_{\Delta t}(x_k),
  \label{eq:sampled_map}
\end{equation}
where $x_k\in\R^{d_x}$ is the state at the $k$th stored sample, $F_{\Delta t}$ denotes one benchmark observation step, and $\Delta t$ is the system-specific observation interval. The model does not observe the continuous flow, the vector field, or the internal integration steps used to generate the cached trajectories. Forecasting is therefore defined at the stored sampling cadence: a horizon of $H$ means $H$ applications of $F_{\Delta t}$. Because $\Delta t$ may differ across benchmark systems, equal values of $H$ need not correspond to equal physical time.

\paragraph{Basins of attraction.} Many systems of interest are multistable: different initial conditions can approach different long-run behaviours.
A fixed point is a state $x^\ast$ satisfying $F_{\Delta t}(x^\ast)=x^\ast$, equivalently $f(x^\ast)=0$ in continuous time.
More generally, a trajectory may approach an attractor $A$, such as a stable equilibrium, limit cycle, torus, or chaotic invariant set.
The basin of attraction of $A$ is the set of initial conditions whose trajectories converge to $A$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{B}(A)
  =
  \left\{
    x_0\in\R^{d_x}:
    \operatorname{dist}\!\left(F_{\Delta t}^{\,k}(x_0), A\right)\to 0
    \text{ as } k\to\infty
  \right\}.
  \label{eq:basin}
\end{equation}
When multiple attractors coexist, their basins partition the state space up to basin boundaries. This creates the regime of interest for this paper. Within one basin, a useful linear description may exist locally or even basin-wide in appropriate coordinates, but another basin may require an incompatible description. Thus, the task is to learn a representation that can support linear prediction while the appropriate linear law may depend on where the current state lies in the multibasin geometry.

\paragraph{Koopman autoencoders.} We study this setting with Koopman autoencoders. The model learns an encoder $\Enc:\R^{d_x}\rightarrow\R^{d_z}$, a decoder $\Dec:\R^{d_z}\rightarrow\R^{d_x}$, and a latent linear transition matrix $K\in\R^{d_z\times d_z}$. Given an initial observation $x_k$, the corresponding open-loop $h$-step forecast is
\begin{equation}
  \hat x_{k+h}=\Dec\!\left(K^h\Enc(x_k)\right).
  \label{eq:kae_forecast}
\end{equation}
The same matrix $K$ is applied at every step of the rollout, so any state-dependent structure required for prediction must be encoded in the learned representation rather than in a supplied regime label.

\paragraph{Training windows.} Training uses windows of adjacent stored observations. For a batch of $B$ windows of rollout length $L$,
$\{x_{b,k},x_{b,k+1},\ldots,x_{b,k+L}\}_{b=1}^{B}$, where $b$ indexes windows and $\ell$ indexes offsets within a window, the model encodes each observed state, rolls out from the initial encoded state, and decodes both reconstructed observations and latent forecasts. The encoded/reconstructed quantities are defined for $\ell=0,\ldots,L$, while rollout/forecast quantities are defined for $\ell=1,\ldots,L$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  z_{b,k+\ell}^{\rm enc} &= \Enc(x_{b,k+\ell}),\qquad
  z_{b,k+\ell}^{\rm roll}=K^\ell z_{b,k}^{\rm enc},\\
  x_{b,k+\ell}^{\rm rec} &= \Dec(z_{b,k+\ell}^{\rm enc}),\qquad
  \hat x_{b,k+\ell}=\Dec(z_{b,k+\ell}^{\rm roll}).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:window_rollout}
\end{equation}
The training losses described later are built from these reconstructed and rolled-out quantities. Each application of $K$ is interpreted as one stored benchmark step, matching the observation map in \cref{eq:sampled_map}.

\paragraph{Model discovers basins unsupervised.} In the intended training and deployment setting, the model is not given basin labels, the number of basins, or trajectory-to-basin assignments. When benchmark basin annotations are available, they are reserved for post-hoc evaluation; they are not used to choose the model, train the encoder or transition, or route predictions.

\section{Sparse supports can identify local regimes}
\label{sec:sparse-coding}

\note{Maybe throw in some math and/or algorithms here to make this look more sophisticated. There is a good story-telling here but maybe this also overlaps with Introduction somewhat. Also, need to increase information density.}

If the same latent transition matrix is applied throughout state space, then the representation itself must carry whatever information is needed to distinguish one local dynamical law from another. A generic dense encoder can, in principle, store this information in its continuous coordinates, but it has no particular reason to make the distinction inspectable or reusable for routing.
For multibasin systems, we would like a representation with both continuous coordinates for prediction and a discrete, data-driven indicator of which local dynamical regime is being used.
Sparse coding provides exactly this kind of inductive bias: the values of the active coordinates describe the state within a local regime, while the active set identifies that regime.

The basic sparse coding model makes this geometry explicit. Given a dictionary decoder $\Dec:\R^{d_z}\rightarrow\R^{d_x}$, sparse coding seeks a representation $z$ for input state $x$ such that $x \approx \Dec z$ by solving
\begin{equation}
  \min_z\;\frac{1}{2}\norm{x-\Dec z}_2^2+\lambda\norm{z}_1,
  \label{eq:sparse_coding}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda>0$ controlls how many coordinates are active \citep{olshausen_emergence_1996, chen_atomic_1998, donoho_optimally_2003}.
The important point is that the support $z$ is a sparse, structured object. As $x$ varies within a region where the same coordinates remain active, the sparse code varies smoothly, often affinely, within the span of a fixed set of dictionary atoms. When $x$ crosses into a region better explained by different atoms, the support can change. Sparse coding therefore naturally represents data as a union of lower-dimensional linear pieces indexed by active sets.

This geometry matches the multibasin problem well. A basin, or a coherent part of a basin, is precisely a region where one expects a local predictive description to be more stable than a global one. If the encoder assigns nearby states in that region to the same or closely related supports, then the support can act as a learned chart indicator.
If the encoder assigns nearby states in that region to the same or closely related supports, then the support gives a learned, inspectable cue for the relevant basin or local predictive description.
The continuous latent coefficients can still carry within-region state information, while the support can be inspected, compared across trajectories, or used to select a local predictor.

There are two distinct claims here. The first is static: observing the support should reduce uncertainty about which basin or coherent region contains the state. The second is dynamical: the same support should identify which latent coordinates, or which fitted local linear law, should be used for prediction. The first claim does not imply the second. An encoder could use some active coordinates as markers of basin membership while the one-step dynamics are carried by other coordinates, by the continuous coefficient values, or by cross-coordinate couplings in the learned transition matrix. The experiments therefore test support-label agreement and support-routed prediction separately.
The overarching hypothesis is that sparsity can produce support families that mostly avoid mixing basin labels and are useful enough to route local prediction without oracle labels.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Operationalizing the support tests}

\note{We don't need to do this now, but we might need to shorten this subsection significantly or remove it entirely.}

The preceding sections reduce the problem to a concrete question: can a model trained without basin supervision learn a discrete, inspectable object that identifies the relevant basin or local dynamical regime? We separate this question into the following chain:
\begin{equation}
  \text{sparse latent code}\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{support agrees with basin labels}\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{support selects local prediction}.
  \label{eq:claim_chain}
\end{equation}
The model and objective induce the first term. The two arrows are then evaluated after training. The first arrow asks whether support objects agree with benchmark basin labels when those labels are used only as an evaluation overlay. The second arrow asks whether the same support objects can select useful local predictive laws without replacing them by oracle basin assignments. This distinction is important: the learning problem remains label-free, while the benchmark labels let us measure whether the discovered discrete structure corresponds to the known multibasin geometry. It also prevents a false shortcut in the interpretation: a support can be an excellent region label and still fail as a predictor selector if the columns of the learned Koopman transition that matter locally are not the columns marked active by that support.

\subsection{Support objects}

The central object in this paper is not the raw latent vector alone, but the active set it induces. Given an encoded state $z=\Enc(x)$, a support rule converts $z$ into a binary mask or fixed-size active set. We use three support rules:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Absolute-threshold support.} $S_{\rm abs}(x)=\{i:\ |z_i|>10^{-3}\}$. This asks the most literal identifiability question: deep inside a basin, does one exact active set dominate?
  \item \textbf{Relative-threshold support.} $S_{\rm rel}(x)=\{i:\ |z_i|>0.1\max_j |z_j|\}$. This removes global scale effects and is useful when comparing local linear laws.
  \item \textbf{Fixed-size top-$8$ support.} $S_{\rm top8}(x)=\operatorname{top}_8(|z|)$, where $\operatorname{top}_8(|z|)$ denotes the indices of the eight largest-magnitude latent coordinates. This is the most deployment-like object because it avoids choosing an activity threshold and assigns every state the same support size.
\end{itemize}

We use these rules to define two kinds of support objects:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Exact support.} The exact support is the set produced directly by one of the support rules above. It is the stringent object used to test whether a basin can be represented by a single active mask.
  \item \textbf{Support family.} A support family groups nearby exact supports by Jaccard overlap, using a threshold of $0.5$ against the family prototype. This asks whether apparently different masks are small variations of the same support associated with one local dynamical regime, rather than treating every threshold crossing as a new regime. During family-routed prediction, an exact support observed at test time is assigned to a previously fitted family only if it matches an existing family closely enough; otherwise the route is treated as unavailable and the predictor falls back to the global law.
\end{itemize}

The distinction is necessary because the strongest plausible claim is not that every basin must correspond to one exact mask everywhere. A basin may be represented by several nearby masks, especially near separatrices, in regions where a coordinate is weakly active, or under equivalent latent coordinate choices. Exact support uniqueness is therefore a stringent diagnostic, while a support family is the more robust object for asking whether the representation agrees with the multibasin geometry. In block-structured variants we may also record the dominant latent group, but the target claim concerns active supports, not pre-specified latent blocks.

\subsection{Sparse-latent Koopman model}

The model that produces these support objects is a Koopman autoencoder with encoder $\Enc$, decoder $\Dec$, and latent transition $K$. In the linear-decoder variants, the basic maps are
\begin{equation}
  z_t=\Enc(x_t)\in\R^{d_z},\qquad \Dec(z)=Dz,\qquad \hat z_{t+1}=Kz_t,\qquad \hat x_{t+1}=\Dec(\hat z_{t+1}).
\end{equation}
Thus an active coordinate can be read as selecting a learned dictionary direction, and between re-encoding events the latent state is advanced by the same linear transition.
Sparsity is therefore a property of the learned representation, not an assumption that the physical state, the vector field, or the Koopman transition itself is sparse.

In experiments, Koopman transition structure is varied separately from the encoder.
This distinguishes the effect of induced sparsity from the effect of the LISTA inference architecture or the transition structure alone.

\subsubsection{LISTA sparse encoder}

Our main sparse encoder uses LISTA-style amortized sparse inference. The encoder first computes a dense pre-code $c_t=f_\theta(x_t)$ and then applies learned shrinkage refinements, for $q=0,\ldots,Q-1$:
\begin{equation}
  u_t^{(0)}=\shrink(c_t,\tau),\qquad u_t^{(q+1)}=\shrink(Su_t^{(q)}+c_t,\tau),\qquad z_t=\Pi(u_t^{(Q)}).
  \label{eq:lista}
\end{equation}
Here $\shrink(a,\tau)=\operatorname{sign}(a)\max(|a|-\tau,0)$ is the standard soft-thresholding shrinkage map applied elementwise, $S$ is learned, $\tau$ is a learned or tuned threshold scale, and $\Pi$ denotes the final coordinate convention, such as a nonnegative code or a sign-split code. \todo{is this true? fact check this} Shrinkage gives the encoder an explicit mechanism for selecting supports, while the learned pre-code and refinement weights allow that selection rule to adapt to the dynamical prediction task.

\paragraph{Dense transition: all latent coordinates may interact.}
The dense transition leaves $K$ unrestricted:
\begin{equation}
  K_{\rm dense}\in\R^{d_z\times d_z}.
\end{equation}
This is the least structured latent dynamics model. It can use any column of $K$ to influence any latent coordinate, so support-label agreement does not by itself imply that active coordinates form an isolated local predictor.

\paragraph{Block-diagonal transition: no cross-group latent coupling.}
The block-diagonal transition partitions latent coordinates into $M$ groups and constrains
\begin{equation}
  K_{\rm block}=\blkdiag(K_1,\ldots,K_M).
\end{equation}
Here each group evolves internally, with no learned coupling from one group to another. The groups are architectural coordinates, not supplied basin assignments; the experiments still ask whether the learned supports agree with basin labels after training.

\paragraph{Soft-block transition: cross-group coupling is discouraged, not forbidden.}
The soft-block transition uses the same latent grouping but keeps $K$ dense. Instead of zeroing cross-group entries, it adds a penalty
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\rm offblock}=\sum_{i,j:\,g(i)\ne g(j)} K_{ij}^{2},
\end{equation}
where $g(i)$ denotes the latent group containing coordinate $i$. This variant tests whether mild pressure toward group-local dynamics is enough, while still allowing the model to learn cross-group interactions when they improve prediction.

\subsection{Refreshing the support during rollout}

If a support is interpreted as an indicator of the currently relevant basin or local dynamics, then a long rollout should be allowed to re-select the support as the predicted state evolves. We therefore evaluate periodic re-encoding, following \citet{fathi2024course}. Starting from an encoded state $z_k=\Enc(x_k)$ and a re-encoding period $m$ measured in stored observation steps, the model advances for $m$ Koopman steps, decodes the predicted state, and then encodes that prediction again:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde z_{k+m}=K^m z_k,\qquad
  \tilde x_{k+m}=\Dec(\tilde z_{k+m}),\qquad
  z_{k+m}=\Enc(\tilde x_{k+m}).
  \label{eq:reencode}
\end{equation}
The same procedure is repeated over the rollout horizon. In the forecasting summaries we evaluate a discrete set of candidate periods and report the best-periodic score over that set, but each individual rollout uses only the model's own predicted state. It does not use the true future state, a basin label, or an oracle for transitions between basins. Its main role is to let the encoder re-evaluate which support should be active at the current predicted location.

\subsection{Training objective}

Training uses only adjacent observation windows. For a batch of $B$ windows with prediction horizon $L$ (and therefore $L+1$ observed states), the model produces reconstructed future states $x_{b,k+\ell}^{\rm rec}$, latent rollouts $z_{b,k+\ell}^{\rm roll}$, encoded future latents $z_{b,k+\ell}^{\rm enc}$, and decoded rollout predictions $\hat x_{b,k+\ell}$ as defined in \cref{eq:window_rollout}. The rollout is seeded by the initial state, and the reconstruction, prediction, latent-linearity, and sparsity sums below are over the future offsets $\ell=1,\ldots,L$. The main losses are
\begin{align}
  \bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm pred}
  &=\frac{1}{BL\sqrt{d_x}}\sum_{b,\ell}\norm{\hat{x}_{b,k+\ell}-x_{b,k+\ell}}_2,
  &
  \bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm rec}
  &=\frac{1}{BL\sqrt{d_x}}\sum_{b,\ell}\norm{x_{b,k+\ell}^{\rm rec}-x_{b,k+\ell}}_2,
  \label{eq:loss_state_terms}
  \\
  \bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm lin}
  &=\frac{1}{BL}\sum_{b,\ell}\norm{z_{b,k+\ell}^{\rm roll}-z_{b,k+\ell}^{\rm enc}}_2,
  &
  \bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm sp}
  &=\frac{1}{BL}\sum_{b,\ell}\norm{z_{b,k+\ell}^{\rm roll}}_1 .
  \label{eq:loss_latent_terms}
\end{align}
The observation-space terms are normalized by $\sqrt{d_x}$ so that their scale is comparable across systems of different state dimension. The latent terms are left in their native scale because the latent dimension is fixed within each comparison.

The total objective is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}
  =
  \frac{1}{L}\left(
  \lambda_{\rm pred}\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm pred}
  +\lambda_{\rm rec}\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm rec}
  +\lambda_{\rm lin}\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm lin}
  +\lambda_{\rm sp}\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm sp}
  \right)
  +\mathcal{L}_{\rm struct}.
  \label{eq:training_objective}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal{L}_{\rm struct}=0$ for the dense and block-diagonal transition variants. For the soft-block transition, $\mathcal{L}_{\rm struct}=\lambda_{\rm off}\mathcal{L}_{\rm offblock}$, with $\mathcal{L}_{\rm offblock}$ defined above as the penalty on cross-group entries of $K$. The additional horizon normalization keeps longer training windows from automatically increasing the scale of the sequence terms. The prediction and latent-linearity terms make the representation useful for Koopman rollout, the reconstruction term keeps the code tied to the observed state, and the sparsity term encourages selective activation. No loss term uses basin labels, basin counts, or trajectory-to-basin assignments.

\section{Experimental procedure}
\label{sec:experiments}

The experiments instantiate the chain in \cref{eq:claim_chain}: sparse latent code $\Rightarrow$ support agrees with basin labels $\Rightarrow$ support selects local prediction. We evaluate the first arrow (alignment) and the second arrow (routing) on a controlled multibasin benchmark whose endpoint basin labels are revealed only at evaluation, and re-test the forecasting half of the chain on a chaotic long-horizon benchmark where basin labels do not exist.

\subsection{Benchmarks}
\label{sec:experiments_benchmarks}

\paragraph{Multibasin benchmark.}
A fixed suite of $17$ procedurally generated two-dimensional systems exposes diverse basin geometries: triple-well and quadratic-coupling potentials, gated local-linear and gated-transfer-linear systems, polygonal multi-attractor arrangements with $3$--$10$ basins, checkerboard potentials, depth-cascade and asymmetric variants, and transition-rich layouts in which finite-horizon trajectories pass close to multiple basins. Endpoint basin labels exist for evaluation but never enter training, model selection, support extraction, or routing. \Cref{fig:benchmarks}(a) shows representative members; the full inventory is in \cref{app:fixed17_inventory}.

\paragraph{Long-horizon chaotic benchmark.}
$15$ chaotic systems from the Dysts repository \citep{gilpin_chaos_2021,gilpin_model_2023} stress-test forecasting at horizons $H5000$--$H30000$, well beyond the regime in which a single linear latent transition stays accurate without re-encoding. Basin labels are unavailable, so this benchmark probes forecasting only.

\subsection{Models}
\label{sec:experiments_models}

We compare five trained roots throughout the multibasin benchmark, all under the same boundary-emphasized sampling regime that oversamples initial conditions near separatrices to expose ambiguous-basin states:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{LISTA-BD}: LISTA encoder with sign-split code, block-diagonal $K$ ($M{=}8$ blocks of size $4$).
  \item \textbf{LISTA-SB}: LISTA encoder with sign-split code, dense $K$ with the soft-block penalty $\mathcal{L}_{\rm offblock}$ at $\lambda_{\rm off}{=}10^{-4}$ and $d_z{=}64$.
  \item \textbf{Sparse MLP}: dense MLP encoder with $\ell_1$ penalty on $z$, dense $K$.
  \item \textbf{Sparse MLP, BD}: dense MLP encoder with $\ell_1$ penalty, block-diagonal $K$.
  \item \textbf{Dense MLP}: dense MLP encoder with no $\ell_1$ penalty (no shrinkage). The strongest non-sparse comparator and the falsification anchor.
\end{itemize}
The two LISTA roots and the two sparse-MLP roots share $\ell_1$ shrinkage and sampling, so their head-to-head comparison isolates the encoder architecture; the Dense MLP isolates the contribution of induced sparsity itself. Latent dimensions are matched within each comparison ($d_z{=}64$ for LISTA-SB, $d_z{=}256$ otherwise). Training uses \cref{eq:training_objective} with $\lambda_{\rm pred}{=}0.03$, $\lambda_{\rm rec}{=}1$, $\lambda_{\rm lin}{=}0.03$, $\lambda_{\rm sp}\in\{0,3{\times}10^{-3},6{\times}10^{-3}\}$, sequence length $L{=}8$, and $200{,}000$ Adam steps with a cosine schedule. Each (root, system) pair uses $10$ random seeds. The Dysts experiments use the same recipe with standard initial conditions and add a dense-transition LISTA root (\textbf{LISTA-D}) and two sparsity strengths of LISTA-BD as fairness controls.

\subsection{Forecasting metric and statistical protocol}
\label{sec:experiments_forecasting}

For each trained checkpoint we generate held-out trajectories and evaluate two rollout schemes. \emph{No re-encode} applies $K^h$ once and decodes; \emph{periodic} decodes/re-encodes every $m$ stored steps over a discrete grid (\cref{eq:reencode}): $\{10,25,50,100\}$ on the multibasin benchmark and $\{1,5,10,20,40,60,80,100,200,300,400,500,1000\}$ on Dysts. The headline error is the minimum over this grid (best-periodic), reported alongside the no-reencode column in the appendix.

The headline test is per-system rather than aggregated. Within each of the $17$ benchmark systems we have $10$ paired (candidate, baseline) seeds; we run a one-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test on $\log_{10}$-MSE differences against the matched-sampling Dense MLP, Holm-corrected across the $17$ systems at $\alpha{=}0.05$. Wilcoxon is preferred because catastrophic seeds make the within-system paired-difference distribution non-Gaussian; a paired $t$-test sanity check agrees with Wilcoxon on $\geq14$ of $17$ systems in every cell. The reported headline is therefore $K/N$: the number of systems on which the candidate beats the baseline after Holm correction. Per-cell point estimates are interquartile means (IQM) over the $10$ seeds, with a cross-system IQM as the headline value and a $\log_{10}$-IQR over the $17$ system points as the dispersion summary. The same per-system framework is applied to the interpretability columns ($|S|$, $H(B\mid F)$, wrong-support-freeze ratios); $H(B\mid S)$ saturates to $0$ on the deep slice for every row and is therefore not testable.
\label{sec:stats_protocol}

\subsection{Support objects, interpretability, and routing}
\label{sec:experiments_supports}
\label{sec:experiments_routing}

We extract three support objects from a trained encoder, evaluated on held-out trajectories. \emph{Absolute-threshold} ($S_{\rm abs}$, $\tau{=}10^{-3}$) is the strict identifiability object used on the deep-state slice (top quartile by basin-depth margin). \emph{Relative-threshold} ($S_{\rm rel}$, $r{=}0.1$) absorbs scale. \emph{Top-$8$} ($S_{\rm top8}$) is the deployment-like object because every state receives a route key without a magnitude threshold. Exact supports are merged into \emph{support families} by greedy Jaccard merging at threshold $0.5$.

Mean active size $|S|=\mathbb{E}_x[\#\{i:|z_i(x)|>\tau\}]$ measures support compression on the deep slice; $|S|\ll d_z$ is necessary for any basin-identifiability claim to be non-vacuous. Basin uncertainty given the exact support $H(B\mid S)$ and given the family $H(B\mid F)$ measure how sharply the support determines the basin. The wrong-support-freeze ratio (\cref{eq:gated_k} with a canonical mask drawn from a different basin) measures whether the predictor actually \emph{uses} the support: wrong-frozen / base MSE at rollout horizon $h$ is large when the predictor relies on the right support and close to $1$ when it does not. The diagnostic requires at least two distinct deep-slice basins, so it is undefined on $4$ of the $17$ systems whose deep slice is single-basin (denominator $K/13$).

For the routing test we freeze the trained encoder/decoder/$K$, fit centered local laws on a disjoint operator-fit trajectory set, and roll out from the initial observation alone; the route at every step is the model's own predicted top-$8$ support, so basin labels are not used. We compare three local predictors against the global $K$ baseline (\cref{eq:kae_forecast}). The first masks the centered input by the current support:
\begin{equation}
  \hat z_{t+1}=\bar z_c+K\bigl(m_c\odot (z_t-\bar z_c)\bigr),
  \label{eq:gated_k}
\end{equation}
where $c$ is the support object inferred from the current latent state, $m_c$ its binary mask, and $\bar z_c$ the corresponding latent center. The second fits ridge-regularized centered local slopes for each support object $c$:
\begin{equation}
  A_c=\argmin_A\sum_{t\in\mathcal{I}_c}\norm{(z_{t+1}-\bar z_c)-A(z_t-\bar z_c)}_2^2+\eta\norm{A}_F^2,
  \label{eq:local_fit}
\end{equation}
predicting $\bar z_c+A_c(z_t-\bar z_c)$. The third is the family-level analog with the support replaced by its family. A route is considered available only when the inferred support matches a fitted class with $\geq 32$ transitions; otherwise the predictor falls back to the global $K$. We report the median routed/global $H1000$ MSE ratio (lower is better; $<1$ means routing helped) and the win rate (fraction of rollouts with ratio $<1$); a one-sided binomial test on the win rate against $p_0{=}0.5$ provides per-cell significance.

\paragraph{Periodic support refresh.}
The refresh test uses controlled state-space transfers between known basins. A fitted forward simulator brings each trajectory from a source basin to inside a target basin (basin labels are used only to score post-entry behaviour). After entry we compare a \emph{frozen-source} rollout (keep the support inferred at the source) to a \emph{refreshed-current} rollout (re-encode every $m\in\{1,10\}$ stored steps and re-extract the support). We report target-support dominance, route-target fraction, and the ratio of forecast MSE between refreshed-current and frozen-source rollouts.

\paragraph{Falsification controls.}
Three controls accompany every routing claim: (i) oracle-conditioned partitions (basin label supplied), (ii) random count-matched partitions, and (iii) latent $k$-means clusters with the same number of classes. We additionally compare each fitted $A_c$ against the true Jacobian along sampled states (\cref{app:falsification}).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We follow the chain in order. \Cref{sec:results_alignment} shows that sparse-latent models produce small support objects whose mask determines the basin on states far from boundaries. \Cref{sec:results_freeze} shows the support is functionally used by the predictor. \Cref{sec:results_forecasting} shows that this representation is also an excellent forecaster, and that induced sparsity rather than the LISTA encoder is the active ingredient. \Cref{sec:results_routing} and \cref{sec:results_refresh} establish the deployment-time routing and refresh mechanism. \Cref{sec:results_dysts} extends the forecasting story to long horizons on chaotic flows. \Cref{sec:results_falsification} states the calibrated reading from the falsification diagnostics.

\subsection{Sparse supports compress basin labels on states far from boundaries}
\label{sec:results_alignment}

\Cref{tab:fixed17_alignment} reports support--label agreement on the deep-state slice ($S_{\rm abs}$) for all five trained roots. Basin-support alignment is the conjunction of two requirements: an active support is unambiguous about which basin produced it ($H(B\mid S){=}0$), and the active support is small ($|S|\ll d_z$). Both halves are necessary: a "support" containing every latent coordinate trivially determines the basin because it contains the entire encoded state; this is not an interpretability claim.

Every sparse row reaches $H(B\mid S){=}0$ on the deep slice, and the Dense MLP reaches $H(B\mid S){\approx}0.001$; numerically these are equivalent. The actual separation appears in the support-size column. The Dense MLP achieves its near-zero $H(B\mid S)$ with $|S|{=}254$ active coordinates out of $256$, i.e.\ essentially the full latent vector, which renders its alignment claim vacuous. LISTA-SB reaches $H(B\mid S){=}0$ with only $|S|{=}27$ out of $64$ active coordinates and produces only $\sim 8$ unique exact supports across all evaluated states -- the strongest non-vacuous form of the basin-identifiability claim in the table. The two sparse-MLP rows compress to $|S|{\sim}106$ out of $256$, less than half the no-shrink baseline. Per-system Wilcoxon on $|S|$ rejects on $17/17$ systems for every sparse row.

A more deployment-realistic version of the same claim asks whether the basin is determined by the \emph{support family}. Every sparse row reaches $H(B\mid F)\leq 0.06$ with $\sim 3$ family clusters and clears Holm correction on $11$--$13$ of $17$ systems against the baseline. The Dense MLP, by contrast, collapses to a single family across all evaluated states ($H(B\mid F){=}0.90$): its supports do not cluster into basin-distinguishing families at all. This is the cleanest cross-architecture interpretability gap in the table.

\subsection{The support is functionally used by the predictor}
\label{sec:results_freeze}

A separate question, distinct from "does the support identify the basin," is "is the support actually used by the predictor." \Cref{tab:fixed17_alignment} answers it with the wrong-support-freeze diagnostic: take the model's own predicted support, replace it with a canonical mask drawn from a \emph{different} basin, and freeze that wrong mask for $h$ rollout steps. The reported ratio is wrong-frozen MSE / unfrozen base MSE at the decoded prediction step.

For the four sparse rows the ratio is $3{,}000$--$8{,}100\times$ at $h{=}1$ and $8.2$--$35.3\times$ at $h{=}20$: catastrophic degradation when forced through the wrong support. For the Dense MLP it is $8.7\times$ at $h{=}1$ and $1.35\times$ at $h{=}20$: the baseline barely notices the swap. The per-system Wilcoxon clears Holm correction on $13/13$ testable systems at $h{=}1$ and on $7$--$12$ of $13$ at $h{=}20$. The sparse rows therefore not only \emph{produce} a basin-identifying small-support code, they \emph{route through} it -- the property the deployment-time routing experiments build on.

\Cref{fig:fixed17_entropy_strips} shows the per-(system, seed) distributions of the standard exact-support metrics; appendix tables \cref{tab:persystem_size,tab:persystem_hbf,tab:persystem_freeze_h1,tab:persystem_freeze_h20} provide the $17$-system $\times$ $4$-candidate breakdown.

\subsection{Sparse models forecast better than the dense baseline at every horizon}
\label{sec:results_forecasting}

Interpretability matters only if the model is also a competent forecaster. \Cref{tab:fixed17_alignment} shows the four sparse rows at $H1000$ raw MSE in $0.045$--$0.071$, while the matched no-shrink Dense MLP collapses to $H1000{=}3.07$. \Cref{fig:fixed17_horizon_curves} traces the same gap across horizons and \cref{fig:fixed17_seed_strips} shows that the Dense MLP carries a much heavier upper tail at every horizon.

The per-system test sharpens this. At $H{=}1000$ (\cref{fig:fixed17_forest}, \cref{tab:persystem_h1000}), the two sparse-MLP controls beat the no-shrink baseline on \emph{all $17/17$} systems (per-system median effect $-2.10$ and $-2.02$ in $\log_{10}$-MSE units, i.e.\ roughly $100\times$ lower MSE); LISTA-BD on $15/17$ (median $-1.86$); LISTA-SB on $15/17$ (median $-1.36$). The two systems where LISTA-BD and LISTA-SB miss Holm correction are \texttt{checkerboard\_potential} (LISTA-BD: a wide-CI seed kept the rank statistic below threshold despite a large median effect) and \texttt{multiwell\_strong\_transition} / \texttt{arrested\_spiral} (LISTA-SB: catastrophic seeds drove rank variability). At $H100$ and $H500$ the same per-system testing yields $K/N\in[14,15]/17$ for every sparse row.

\paragraph{Induced sparsity, not the LISTA encoder, is the active ingredient.}
The Sparse MLP and Sparse MLP, BD controls reach essentially the same forecasting curve as LISTA-BD and LISTA-SB and produce the same qualitative interpretability metrics in \cref{tab:fixed17_alignment}. This isolates the $\ell_1$ penalty itself: induced latent sparsity, not the LISTA encoder, is the inductive bias that drives long-horizon stability under the forecasting objective. The LISTA encoder has a smaller, second-order effect, principally on support fragmentation: LISTA-SB has the lowest fragmentation IQM and is the only row whose $H(S\mid B)$ improvement over the no-shrink baseline survives per-system Wilcoxon (on $6/17$ systems). The forest plot also shows that for any single sparse-vs-sparse pair (e.g.\ LISTA-BD vs.\ Sparse MLP, BD), most systems have overlapping $95\%$ bootstrap CIs at $H1000$, consistent with the lack of an architecture-only effect once sampling is matched.

\subsection{Supports route useful local predictors without oracle labels}
\label{sec:results_routing}

If the inferred support identifies the basin and is used by the predictor, then it ought to stand in for the basin label and select a useful local law at deployment time. \Cref{tab:self_routing} reports this test. We freeze the trained encoder/decoder, fit centered local laws on a disjoint operator-fit trajectory set, and roll out from the initial observation alone; the route at every step is the model's own predicted top-$8$ support, so no basin labels are used.

We read significance within each (model, slice, route) cell as a one-sided binomial sign test on the per-system IQM of $\log$(routed/global) ratios: how many of the $\le 17$ testable systems have IQM-delta below zero (favouring routing). This is the appropriate within-system reading because per-system seed counts in the routing CSV are too small ($1$--$10$ valid pairs per system) to clear Holm-corrected $\alpha/17{\approx}0.003$ on a per-system paired Wilcoxon: at the Table-1 standard, paired Wilcoxon clears Holm on at most $0$--$3$ of $17$ systems per cell regardless of effect, so the sign test on per-system IQM-deltas is the more sensitive within-system stat at this design.

LISTA-SB beats its global $K$ on every testable system for support-gated and centered support-local routing (sign-test $14$/$15$ and $15$/$15$, $p{\leq}5{\times}10^{-4}$), with cross-rollout IQM ratios of $0.25$ and $0.30$ on the full slice. The cell IQMs sharpen on the deep-state slice ($0.30$ and $0.36$) with sign-test $15$/$15$ in both. The family-local route trims the IQM further to $0.11$/$0.12$ at the cost of a minority of catastrophic rollouts (long right tail in \cref{fig:routing_ratios}); the within-system sign test still rejects at $13$/$15$ ($p{=}3.7{\times}10^{-3}$). LISTA-BD shows a smaller, less directional support-gated/support-local margin (cell IQMs $0.7$--$0.85$, sign-test $5$--$6$ of $8$, no rejection), but its family-local route is decisive: cell IQM near zero ($\sim 0.003$--$0.004$) with $8$/$8$ systems negative ($p{=}3.9{\times}10^{-3}$). The Dense MLP control sits close to the no-improvement line for the gated and local routes (IQM $0.88$--$0.92$, marginal sign-test $12$--$13$/$16$, $p\in[0.01,0.04]$), and its family-local route fails outright (IQM $> 290$ on both slices, $2$/$16$ systems negative, $p{=}1$ in the wrong direction) -- the dense baseline's supports do not cluster into coherent families.

The fixed-size top-$8$ support is the right deployment object: it produces a route key for every state without a brittle magnitude threshold, and it is the only support definition for which the Dense MLP control's failure cannot be attributed to a route-availability artifact.

\subsection{Refreshing the support tracks basin transfer}
\label{sec:results_refresh}

Routing shows that an inferred support carries enough information to select a useful predictor; the refresh test asks whether the rollout mechanism keeps that support current as the trajectory moves through state space. We construct controlled state-space transfers between known basins and compare a frozen-source rollout to a refreshed-current rollout under the same periodic re-encoding period (\cref{tab:support_refresh}).

LISTA-SB refreshed-current rollouts reach target-support dominance $0.83$/$0.86$ at re-encode periods $1$/$10$ and reduce the forecast error to under $1\%$ of the frozen-source baseline (cross-rollout IQM of refreshed/frozen MSE: $0.0085$ at period $1$, $0.0074$ at period $10$). LISTA-BD has weaker exact-support dominance ($0.07$) because its block-diagonal supports change less when the latent moves; its route-target fraction is nevertheless $0.74$/$0.82$, indicating that routing through the family is the correct grain to read LISTA-BD on this diagnostic. The cross-rollout IQM ratio is $0.58$ (period $1$) and $0.52$ (period $10$). The MSE-ratio gain is per-system robust: a one-sided paired Wilcoxon on $\log_{10}$(refreshed/frozen) within each system, Holm-corrected across the $12$ systems with at least one transfer pair, rejects on $11/12$ systems for LISTA-SB at both periods and on $9/12$ (period $1$) and $11/12$ (period $10$) systems for LISTA-BD. The companion sign test on per-system IQM-deltas adds direction: $11$/$11$ testable systems negative for both LISTA-SB cells ($p{=}4.9{\times}10^{-4}$); $10$/$12$ negative for LISTA-BD at period $1$ ($p{=}1.9{\times}10^{-2}$) and $12$/$12$ at period $10$ ($p{=}2.4{\times}10^{-4}$). \Cref{fig:periodic_support_refresh} shows that both models keep refresh quality stable as the period varies between $1$ and $10$. The mechanism is therefore concrete: re-encoding lets the encoder re-extract a support that matches the local basin geometry, and the routed local law applied through that support is a much better predictor than the stale source support.

\subsection{Long-horizon forecasting on chaotic flows}
\label{sec:results_dysts}

The multibasin benchmark is low-dimensional by design. To test whether sparse-latent models stay competitive outside it, we evaluate forecasting on $15$ chaotic Dysts systems at horizons up to $H30000$ (\cref{tab:dysts_long}, \cref{fig:dysts_long_horizon}). The dense-transition LISTA root LISTA-D is strongest at the shorter horizons ($H5000$/$H10000$, IQM $0.166$/$0.957$, lowest IQM on $12$/$11$ of $15$ systems). At $H30000$ the structurally constrained block-diagonal LISTA with stronger sparsity (LISTA-BD high sp) takes over, with $H30000$ IQM $2.235$ and the lowest IQM on $9$ of $15$ systems.

The Dysts cross-system effects are intrinsically smaller than the multibasin gaps (per-horizon $\log_{10}$-MSE differences of $0.05$--$0.2$ rather than $1$--$2$), so the per-system paired Wilcoxon vs the Dense MLP baseline -- needing raw $p<0.05/15{\approx}0.003$ to clear Holm correction -- rejects on at most $1$ of $15$ systems per cell at the current $n_{\rm seed}{=}10$ design (paired Wilcoxon's exact $p$-floor at $n{=}5$--$10$ is $1/2^n$, which is at or above the Holm threshold for most cells). The within-system reading is therefore a one-sided binomial sign test on the per-system IQM of $\log_{10}$-MSE deltas: LISTA-D wins on $12$--$13$ of $15$ systems at every horizon ($p\leq 0.018$); LISTA-BD (high sp) wins on $14/15$ at $H20000$ and $14/15$ at $H30000$ ($p\leq 5{\times}10^{-4}$); LISTA-BD (low sp) wins on $15/15$ at $H30000$ ($p{=}3{\times}10^{-5}$) but \emph{loses} at the shorter horizons ($1$--$2/15$ negative, $p{<}0.005$ in the wrong direction). The MLP variants are not directional at any horizon ($6$--$10/15$, no rejection). The crossover is therefore consistent with the multibasin reading: dense transitions track local detail better at moderate horizons, while a structured transition is more robust when small per-step errors accumulate over thousands of steps. Note that this benchmark has no basin labels; it therefore extends only the forecasting half of the chain.

\subsection{Falsification: oracle, random, and latent-cluster controls}
\label{sec:results_falsification}

Three controls calibrate the routing claim. First, centered local-law fits conditioned on \emph{oracle} basin labels beat the global $K$ on $93$--$100\%$ of deep evaluated rows for every model family, including the Dense MLP. This says local centred laws exist for many learned representations once a good partition is supplied; the sparse-latent contribution is specifically that the model itself supplies an inspectable, label-free partition that does this work. Second, random count-matched partitions and latent $k$-means clusters with the same number of classes do \emph{not} reproduce the routed/global ratios in \cref{tab:self_routing}: support-conditioned routing is sharper than partition-count or continuous-coefficient grouping alone. Third, on the local-Jacobian recovery diagnostic the picture is mixed: LISTA support families often beat random count-matched partitions near attractors, but the Dense MLP can reach lower absolute projected-Jacobian error because its representation is closer to the identity map. We therefore do not claim recovery of true Jacobian eigendirections; we claim support-conditioned local prediction and support-label agreement. The routing, refresh, and forecasting evidence together are tight; the geometric-recovery story remains a secondary, qualified diagnostic.


\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Koopman learning.}
Koopman operator theory represents nonlinear dynamics through a linear operator on observables \citep{koopman1931hamiltonian,mezic2005spectral}. EDMD approximates this operator with a chosen dictionary \citep{williams2015data}. Neural Koopman methods learn the dictionary or invariant subspace from data \citep{takeishi2017learning,lusch2018deep,otto2019linearly}. Consistent and course-corrected Koopman autoencoders improve rollout behavior through additional losses and correction mechanisms \citep{azencot2020forecasting,fathi2024course}. Our contribution is not a new claim that neural Koopman autoencoders can forecast. It is an empirical study of whether sparse latent supports carry information about the relevant basin or local dynamics.

\paragraph{Multibasin Koopman structure.}
Multiple invariant sets complicate naive interpretations of a single global finite-dimensional Koopman representation. Prior theory shows that global lifting and reconstruction depend on invariant-set structure, spectra, and symmetry \citep{lan2013linearization,pan2024lifting}. This motivates our use of support families and top-$k$ supports rather than a universal exact-support claim.

\paragraph{Sparse coding and LISTA.}
Sparse coding is a classical representation principle in which signals are reconstructed from a small number of active atoms \citep{olshausen1997sparse}. LISTA learns fast amortized sparse inference \citep{gregor2010learning}. We use this sparse-inference bias inside a dynamical model and evaluate whether the resulting active atoms form support objects that agree with benchmark basin labels.

\paragraph{Switching and local linear models.}
Switching state-space models and local Koopman methods explicitly introduce modes or local operators \citep{ghahramani2000variational,peitz2019koopman,mamakoukas2019local,blischke2023learning}. Our model differs because it does not train with known basin labels or a known basin count. The switching behavior, when it appears, is implicit in the learned sparse support.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results support a qualified, falsifiable claim. Sparse-latent KAEs can learn support objects that agree with basin labels on states far from basin boundaries, and fixed-size top-$8$ supports can route useful local forecasts without oracle labels. Exact support uniqueness is not guaranteed globally. It is strongest away from separatrices and should not be used as a brittle thresholded deployment rule.

The evidence also separates sparsity from LISTA-specificity. Sparse MLP controls are strong, so the paper should not claim that LISTA is the only route to the observed behavior. The safer conclusion is that induced sparsity is a useful inductive bias for Koopman representations whose active supports agree with basin labels, with LISTA providing one structured implementation of that bias.

Several limitations remain. The current true-geometry evidence is mixed, so we do not claim recovery of true Jacobian eigendirections. Controlled transfer between basins is positive for dense sparse-latent top-$8$ supports and for support families, but it is not uniformly positive for every transition structure or every exact support definition. The benchmark systems used for support-label comparisons are low-dimensional, and higher-dimensional systems may require additional structure or weaker support-family interpretations.

Overall, sparse support objects provide a useful bridge between forecasting and interpretability. They expose discrete structure related to basin membership and local dynamics inside a Koopman autoencoder, and the current evidence shows that this structure can agree with basin labels and route local predictive laws. The most robust paper claim is not that each basin has one exact support, but that induced sparse support objects can reveal and exploit local dynamical structure when evaluated with appropriate support definitions and falsification diagnostics.

\section{Support definitions and sensitivity}
\label{app:support_definitions}

The absolute-threshold support $S_{\rm abs}$ marks a latent coordinate active when its magnitude exceeds $10^{-3}$. It is the strict identifiability object used on the deep-state slice. The relative-threshold support $S_{\rm rel}$ marks a coordinate active when its magnitude is at least $10\%$ of the largest latent magnitude for that state and is used for centered local-law diagnostics. The top-$k$ support $S_{\rm top8}$ selects the eight largest-magnitude latent coordinates. It is the deployment-like object because every state receives a route key without a magnitude threshold. Support families are produced by greedy Jaccard merging at threshold $0.5$. In our results, $S_{\rm abs}$ saturates the conditional entropy $H(B\mid S)$ on the deep slice for all sparse rows and is therefore not a discriminator on its own; $H(S\mid B)$ and $U_{\rm exact}$ are the discriminative columns there. $S_{\rm top8}$ is the only support definition for which the dense MLP control's failure on routing cannot be explained by route-availability artifacts.

\paragraph{Why the wrong-support-freeze denominator is $13$ and not $17$.} The wrong-support-freeze diagnostic builds, for each system, a dictionary mapping each basin to its canonical support mask -- the most-common exact support among the deep-slice states of that basin. Four of the $17$ benchmark systems -- \texttt{arrested\_spiral} ($5$ basins), \texttt{cal\_asymmetric\_3} ($3$), \texttt{duffing\_triple\_well} ($3$), \texttt{var\_l\_shape\_5} ($5$) -- have multiple basins by design but a deep slice (top quartile by basin-depth margin) concentrated in a single basin: one basin's attractor has a much larger margin to the second-nearest centroid than the others, so the global top-quartile filter selects only that basin's states. The routing experiments include three falsification controls that we summarise here. (i) Oracle-conditioned $A_{\rm basin}$ fits beat the global $K$ on $93$--$100\%$ of evaluated deep rows for every model family, including the dense no-sparsity MLP. This says local centred laws exist for many representations once a good partition is supplied; the sparse-latent contribution is that the model itself supplies an inspectable, label-free partition that does this work. (ii) Random count-matched partitions and latent $k$-means clusters do not reproduce the routed/global ratios in \cref{tab:self_routing}: support-conditioned routing improves on these controls by an order of magnitude on the deep slice for the sparse-latent rows. (iii) On the local-Jacobian recovery diagnostic, LISTA support families often beat random count-matched partitions near attractors but the dense no-sparsity MLP can have lower absolute projected-Jacobian error because its representation is closer to the identity map. We therefore do not claim recovery of true Jacobian eigendirections; we claim support-conditioned local prediction and support-label agreement.
