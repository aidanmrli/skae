\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Learning accurate and interpretable models of nonlinear dynamical systems remains a central problem in machine learning, scientific computing, and control. One particularly attractive direction is to seek a representation in which the nonlinear dynamics can become linear, since linear models support efficient prediction, estimation, and control while remaining amenable to classical solving tools such as linear quadratic regulators (LQR) \citep{kalman_general_1960} and model predictive control (MPC) \citep{mayne_constrained_2000, grune_nonlinear_2017}. Koopman operator theory provides this perspective: instead of evolving the state in the nonlinear dynamical system directly, it studies the evolution of observables under a linear operator acting on a lifted function space \citep{koopman_hamiltonian_1931, koopman_dynamical_1932, brunton_modern_2022}. This viewpoint has inspired a large line of literature on data-driven approximations of Koopman dynamics, such as DMD and eDMD \citep{schmid_dmd_2010, williams_datadriven_2015, williams_kernel-based_2016}, and applications of these linear predictors for nonlinear control via MPC \citep{korda_linear_2018} or LQR \citep{mamakoukas_local_2019}.

The difficulty is that the most interesting nonlinear systems typically contain multiple equilibria or multiple basins of attraction, and theory shows that these systems cannot admit a single finite-dimensional global linearization \citep{lan_linearization_2013, brunton_koopman_2016}. 
In a multistable finite-dimensional system, it is possible to linearize the dynamics within each basin of attraction, but the linearizations needed in different basins of attraction are incompatible with each other. The problem goes beyond predicting trajectories within a single locally valid basin to representing dynamics that may move across qualitatively different regimes while preserving enough structure to support downstream reasoning and control.

Recent studies have merged Koopman theory with deep learning. Deep autoencoder-based models learn nonlinear observables jointly with latent linear dynamics, improving flexibility over hand-designed lifting functions \citep{lusch_deep_2018, azencot_forecasting_2020, shi_deep_2022}. However, unrolling the linear dynamics in latent space over long rollouts leads to drift due to crossing of long-horizon trajectories and inability of latent space trajectory generation to capture switching dynamics between fixed points \citep{fathi_course_2023}. \citet{fathi_course_2023} proposed periodic re-encoding, which maps (decodes) the lifted latent back to the space of observables (state space) and immediately re-encodes the decoded state back to the lifted latent space. Periodic re-encoding was shown to be an effective inference-time correction mechanism, and therefore is a natural starting point for studying multistable dynamics.

However, long-horizon prediction is only half of the story. If Koopman models are to serve as a foundation for interpretable multi-regime modeling, then the latent representation should do more than forecast well: ideally, it should organize the state space into meaningful local dynamical regimes. More generally, if one finite-dimensional latent is asked to represent several basins or fixed points at once, then some mechanism that induces sparse and selective latent supports may be essential so that different local linearizations do not interfere destructively. Classical sparse coding and LISTA-style encoders provide one principled way to impose that inductive bias, since they produce piecewise-linear mappings and union-of-subspaces structure in latent space \citep{olshausen_emergence_1996, chen_atomic_1998, donoho_optimally_2003, gregor_learning_2010}. In principle, the support of a sparse latent code could act as a mode variable, indicating which local linear model is active and how trajectories transition between regimes. LISTA is therefore best viewed here as one structured realization of a broader sparsity hypothesis, not as the claim by itself.

This idea is conceptually compelling, but it is not yet an empirical fact. A sparse support can align with basin structure, but it can also fragment trajectories in ways that have little to do with basins of attraction.
We study this gap directly, and ask the following question: given only unlabeled trajectories from nonlinear systems with multiple basins of attraction, can a sparse Koopman autoencoder simultaneously provide accurate long-horizon forecasting under periodic re-encoding and a latent support structure that aligns with basin geometry?

\paragraph{Contributions.}
We make two contributions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item We study whether induced sparsity is a necessary ingredient for good finite-dimensional Koopman representations in multi-basin systems by comparing LISTA encoders, sparsity-regularized MLP encoders, and no-shrink MLP controls under matched periodic re-encoding protocols.
    \item We evaluate whether sparse latent supports align with basin structure, and whether forecasting gains concentrate deep inside a basin rather than near a separatrix, showing that basin-support alignment is a conditional phenomenon rather than a universal property of sparse Koopman representations.
\end{itemize}

In \cref{sec:method}, we describe the problem formulation, our methodology in sparse coding and periodic re-encoding, and training objectives. 
In \cref{sec:experiments}, we provide a fair empirical comparison of the forecasting performance of MLP encoders versus LISTA encoders with explicit sparsity objectives under periodic re-encoding.
Finally, we wrap up with a brief discussion detailing future work in \cref{sec:discussion}. Appendix~\ref{sec:background} provides a detailed background reading and Appendix~\ref{appendix:experimental-protocol} provides detailed experimental protocol for reproducibility.

\label{sec:koopman-background}

This subsection largely follows the review of \citet{brunton_modern_2022} with some changes in notation.

\paragraph{Koopman operator and observables.}
Consider a discrete-time nonlinear dynamical system
\begin{equation}
x_{t+1} = f(x_t), \qquad x_t \in \mathcal{X} \subset \mathbb{R}^{d_x},
\end{equation}
where $x_t$ is the state and $f : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{X}$ describes the dynamics.
Koopman operator theory studies the evolution of \emph{observables}, which are measurement functions $h : \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}$.
The Koopman operator $\mathcal{K}$ is defined by composition:
\begin{equation}
(\mathcal{K}h)(x) = h(f(x)).
\end{equation}
Although $f$ may be nonlinear, $\mathcal{K}$ is always linear on the (typically infinite-dimensional) function space of observables. Then, instead of evolving the state $x_t$ directly, we can evolve a vector of observables
\begin{equation}
\phi(x) = \big[h_1(x), \dots, h_{d_z}(x)\big]^\top.
\end{equation}
If the span of $\{h_1,\dots,h_{d_z}\}$ is invariant under $\mathcal{K}$, 
then there exists a matrix
$K \in \mathbb{R}^{d_z \times d_z}$ such that
\begin{equation}
\phi(x_{t+1}) = \phi(f(x_t)) = \mathcal{K}\phi(x_t) = K\, \phi(x_t),
\end{equation}
so that the nonlinear dynamics of $x_t$ are exactly represented by a linear dynamical system
in the observable coordinates $z_t = \phi(x_t)$.
To learn linear latent-state models of nonlinear dynamics, the goal is to find observables that approximately span such a Koopman-invariant subspace.

\paragraph{Approximating the Koopman operator: DMD/eDMD.}
In practice, we do not have access to the infinite-dimensional Koopman operator, and we must work with a finite family of observables.
Given a feature map $\phi : \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}^{d_z}$, one seeks a finite-dimensional approximation
\begin{equation}
\phi(x_{t+1}) \approx K\, \phi(x_t),
\end{equation}
where $K \in \mathbb{R}^{d_z \times d_z}$ is a learned linear model.
Data-driven approximations such as dynamic mode decomposition (DMD) \citep{schmid_dmd_2010, rowley_2009_spectral, tu_dynamic_2014} and extended DMD (eDMD) \citep{williams_datadriven_2015, williams_kernel-based_2016} estimate $K$ from pairs $\{(x_t, x_{t+1})\}$ by solving
\begin{equation}
K = \arg\min_{\hat K} \sum_{t} \big\| \phi(x_{t+1}) - \hat K\, \phi(x_t) \big\|_2^2,
\end{equation}
with $\phi$ chosen from a (possibly large) dictionary of basis functions.
If the chosen observables are approximately invariant under $\mathcal{K}$, then the learned $K$ captures a
nearly linear evolution of the feature vector $z_t = \phi(x_t)$ while still allowing rich nonlinear behaviour
in the original state $x_t$.

DMD is a special case: we take $\phi(x)=x$ itself, so DMD directly fits a linear map
\begin{equation}
K^{\mathrm{DMD}} = \arg\min_{\hat K} \sum_{t} \big\| x_{t+1} - \hat K x_t \big\|_2^2
\end{equation}
to the state.
This can be viewed as learning a representation in which the transition model is as close as possible to linear while still capturing important nonlinearity in the observed trajectories.

\paragraph{Problems with multiple basins of attraction.}

While Koopman representations are powerful, they face theoretical limitations in systems with complex global topology. A key concept here is the \emph{basin of attraction}. First, an \emph{attractor} $A$ is a set of points that has the property of asymptotic stability: trajectories that start near the set approach it asymptotically as $t \to \infty$ 
(e.g. a \emph{fixed point} $x^\ast$ that satisfies $f(x^\ast) = x^\ast$). Then, the basin of attraction $\mathcal{B}(A)$ is the set of initial conditions whose trajectories approach the attractor $A$ in the long-time limit:
\begin{equation} 
\mathcal{B}(A) = \{ x \in \mathcal{X} : \lim_{t \to \infty} \text{dist}(f^t(x), A) = 0 \}.
\end{equation}

For many nonlinear systems, especially those with multiple basins of attraction, no finite-dimensional Koopman-invariant subspace exists that both (1) contains the original state coordinates $x$, and (2) is globally invariant under $\mathcal{K}$ in a way that yields exact dynamics on all basins simultaneously.

\citet{lan_linearization_2013}, also summarized in \citet{brunton_koopman_2016}, show that such finite-dimensional invariant subspaces containing the state can exist only for systems with a single isolated fixed point whose basin covers the state space.
% If a nonlinear system has more than one isolated fixed point, it cannot admit a finite-dimensional Koopman-invariant subspace that both spans the state and yields exact global dynamics.
% However, for each \emph{single} basin of attraction, we can linearize the dynamics within that basin nicely.
% Near a hyperbolic fixed point, classical linearization theory guarantees the existence of local coordinates in which the dynamics are well-approximated by a linear system, and Koopman eigenfunctions provide one way to construct such local coordinates.
% The only struggle is: how do we glue these separate basins, where we can linearize the dynamics separately, into one finite-dimensional global system that covers all basins and still keeps the dynamics linear and the map from latent $z$ to state $x$?
Any kind of finite-dimensional Koopman-learned latent for a multi-basin system must either:
\begin{itemize}
    \item be effectively \emph{piecewise linear}, where the latent includes some implicit ``which basin am I in?'' variable, or
    \item be \emph{approximate}, in the sense that it smooths over the boundaries between regions with different dynamics and is not exact anywhere.
\end{itemize}
This observation motivates our use of sparse coding to make explicit which local model is active, and periodic re-encoding to switch between locally valid linearizations as trajectories move across basin boundaries.



\paragraph{Koopman predictors for controlled systems (optional).}
For controlled systems
\begin{equation}
x_{t+1} = f(x_t, u_t),
\end{equation}
Koopman-based approaches often add controls to the higher-dimensional latent where the dynamics are linear:
\begin{equation}
z_{t+1} = A z_t + B u_t, \qquad x_t \approx C z_t,
\end{equation}
where $z_t = \psi(x_t)$ is a vector of learned observables, and $A,B,C$ are learned from data \citep{korda_linear_2018}.
This predictor has linear dynamics in the lifted space, but the feedback law $u_t = \kappa_{\mathrm{lift}}(z_t)$ induces a nonlinear controller $\kappa(x_t) = \kappa_{\mathrm{lift}}(\psi(x_t))$ in the original state.
Such linear predictors can be plugged into standard tools such as LQR and MPC while the nonlinearity is hidden in the fixed lifting map $x \mapsto z$ and decoder.

There are many benefits to having linear dynamics in the latent space.
Firstly, linear dynamics allow for LQR solvers to be applied to derive closed-form solutions to optimal control problems \citep{kalman_general_1960}.
Linear dynamics also significantly simplify system identification and interpretability, and can be efficiently unrolled with parallelism (e.g., in modern state-space models \citep{gu2022efficiently, gu_2023_mamba, smith2023simplified, dao_2024_mamba2}), which is attractive for long-horizon prediction and planning.



\subsection{Koopman autoencoders and periodic re-encoding}
\label{sec:background-reencoding}

Recent work \citep{lusch_deep_2018, azencot_forecasting_2020, shi_deep_2022, frion_leveraging_2023} has combined Koopman operator theory with deep autoencoders to learn nonlinear observables and linear latent dynamics directly from data, similar to DMD and eDMD.
In a typical Koopman autoencoder, an encoder $\phi : \R^{d_x} \to \R^{d_z}$ and decoder $\psi : \R^{d_z} \to \R^{d_x}$ are trained together with a linear latent Koopman transition $K$ so that
\begin{equation}
z_t \approx \phi(x_t), \qquad z_{t+1} \approx K z_t, \qquad x_t \approx \psi(z_t).
\end{equation}
We might do this by minimizing the following objective:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:KAE-standard-objective}
\min_{K, \phi, \psi} \sum_\mathcal{D} ||x_t - \psi(\phi(x_t)) ||_2 + \lambda \cdot || \phi(x_{t+1}) - K \phi(x_t)||_2
\end{equation} 
where $\lambda > 0$ is a scalar.
Once trained, one can simulate forward in time by iterating the linear latent dynamics and decoding:
\begin{equation}
z_{t+k} \approx K^k z_t, \qquad \hat x_{t+k} = \psi(z_{t+k}).
\end{equation}

\citet{fathi_course_2023} observed that unrolling the linear dynamics in latent space in this way leads to long-term drift in trajectories when mapped back to the state space.
Because the encoder and decoder are not exact inverses and the Koopman-invariance of the learned features is only approximate, latent trajectories $z_t$ can slowly drift away from the region where the local linear approximation is valid, resulting in trajectories that cross or develop spurious attractors when decoded to $x$.

To address this, they introduced \emph{periodic re-encoding}.
Rather than letting the latent state evolve indefinitely under $K$, one intermittently decodes and re-encodes:
\begin{equation}
z_{t+k} = \phi\!\left(\psi\!\left(K^k z_t\right)\right),
\end{equation}
for some re-encoding period $k$.
More concretely, the trajectory is evolved linearly in the latent space for several steps, decoded back to the original state, and then this decoded state is re-encoded to obtain a fresh latent representation.
This contrasts with a one-shot encoding at the initial time, where the same linear approximation is used regardless of how far the trajectory has moved in state space.

In the context of the result by \citet{lan_linearization_2013}, periodic re-encoding can be understood as a mechanism for switching between locally-valid linearizations as the dynamics traverse different regions or cross basin boundaries.
Within a single basin away from the boundaries, the encoder–decoder pair should behave approximately linearly along trajectories, but near basin boundaries, the geometry of invariant sets and eigenfunctions changes rapidly: observables that were approximately Koopman-invariant in one region may no longer span an invariant subspace in the new region.
By re-encoding at these times (or at a fixed period that is short compared to the time spent near boundaries), the model effectively refreshes the latent embedding so that the latent linear dynamics are again centered on the current part of the attractor or basin. This should help the system switch to a new linearized representation of the new basin.

\subsection{Sparse coding}
\label{sec:background-sparse}

Sparse coding seeks a representation $z$ such that $x \approx Dz$ for a learned dictionary $D : \R^{d_z} \to \R^{d_x}$, via the optimization
\begin{equation}
\min_z \;\; \frac{1}{2} \|x - D z\|_2^2 + \lambda \|z\|_1,
\end{equation}
where $\lambda > 0$ controls the sparsity of $z$ \citep{olshausen_emergence_1996, chen_atomic_1998, donoho_optimally_2003}.
The $\ell_1$ penalty induces a mapping $x \mapsto z(x)$ that is piecewise linear and piecewise constant in its support: on each region of input space, the same small set of dictionary atoms is active, and within that region, $z(x)$ depends linearly on $x$.
Geometrically, this models the latent space as a union of lower-dimensional linear subspaces, with each subspace indexed by the support (active set) of the sparse code.

At a high level, this union-of-subspaces structure is exactly the kind of structure we would like to impose on a multi-basin nonlinear control problem.
Each support pattern corresponds to a locally linear regime; moving between regimes corresponds to changing which atoms are active.

In our setting, we want to treat the Koopman features as a sparse combination of dictionary atoms.
The dictionary $D$ is global, and the support pattern of the sparse code $z$ acts like a mode label indicating which basin or local region we are currently in.
Inside a region (basin of attraction) where the dynamics look locally linear, that region uses the same dictionary atoms and therefore has its own local Koopman-like dynamics.
Crossing a basin boundary is equivalent to changing the sparse code support to another region with a different local linear model. I felt that this was also explained nicely in Appendix D of \citet{fathi_course_2023}.

This gives a natural way to build an adjacency matrix for a graph that encapsulates the nonlinear problem: nodes correspond to local regions of constant support, and edges correspond to observed transitions between supports along trajectories.
On each node, one can learn a local Koopman linear operator and solve the associated LQR-style control problem in closed form.
Planning trajectories from one side of the field to the other then becomes a graph traversal problem over these sparse-code regions.

\paragraph{LISTA as a fast sparse encoder.}
Classical sparse coding solves the above optimization with an iterative algorithm (e.g., ISTA/FISTA, coordinate descent), which is often too slow to use inside a learned dynamical model.
LISTA (Learned ISTA) \citep{gregor_learning_2010} replaces the iterative solver with a trainable, finite-depth network that approximates the sparse solution.

In LISTA, one learns a non-linear encoder $z = f_\theta(x)$ whose architecture mimics a truncated iterative shrinkage/thresholding algorithm.
The key nonlinearity is a component-wise shrinkage function with learned thresholds; by construction, the mapping $x \mapsto z(x)$ is explicitly piecewise affine, with regions defined by which coordinates survive thresholding.
This builds a strong inductive bias for \emph{mode selection} into the encoder: for each $x$, only a few coordinates of $z$ are active, picking a small subset of dictionary atoms that explain the input.
Compared to a generic nonlinear encoder where all latent coordinates can participate everywhere, LISTA-style encoders encourage a sparse, structured partition of state space into locally linear regimes, which is exactly what we will leverage in our Koopman control architecture.


\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{LISTA Encoder (Forward Pass)}
\label{alg:lista}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE $x \in \mathbb{R}^{d_x}$; depth $T \in \mathbb{N}$; encoder $\phi$ with learnable parameters $W_e \in \mathbb{R}^{d_z \times d_x}$, $S \in \mathbb{R}^{d_z \times d_z}$, $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^{d_z}_{\ge 0}$
\ENSURE Output $z \in \mathbb{R}^{d_z}$ ($z$ is latent code $\phi(x)$)
\STATE $z^{(0)} \gets h_\theta (W_e x)$
\FOR{$t = 1$ \TO $T$}
    \STATE $c^{(t)} \gets W_e x + Sz^{(t-1)}$ 
    \STATE $z^{(t)} \gets \operatorname{h}_{\theta} \big(c^{(t)}\big)$
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $z^{(T)} = \phi(x)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

We represent the decoder as a linear dictionary $D \in \mathbb{R}^{d_x \times d_z}$, so that $\hat{x} = D z$. The encoder $\phi(x)$ is implemented as a LISTA network (Alg. \ref{alg:lista}) with parameters $W_e \in \mathbb{R}^{d_z \times d_x}, S \in \mathbb{R}^{d_z \times d_z}$, and thresholds $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^{d_z}_{\ge 0}$; here $W_e$ plays the role of a learned operator analogous to $D^\top$, while $D$ is the dictionary used in reconstruction.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

We study Koopman autoencoders in the setting that is most relevant for multistable dynamics: trajectories are generated by systems with multiple basins of attraction, but neither the number of basins nor the basin assignment of each state is available during training or deployment. 
% The goal is for the Koopman autoencoder to learn representations that support accurate long-horizon forecasting under periodic re-encoding. 
In this regime, a useful Koopman representation outputted from the encoder should satisfy two requirements. 
First, the representations must support accurate long-horizon forecasting under periodic re-encoding. 
Second, its sparse support pattern should organize trajectories by local dynamical regime without requiring supervised mode labels.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

For each benchmark system, the underlying state evolves in continuous time according to an unknown flow or, equivalently for our purposes, a simulator-defined one-step map.  
The learned model does not observe the continuous flow directly; instead it receives a sequence of sampled states \(x_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d_x}\), \(k=0,1,2,\dots\), related by 
$x_{k+1} = F_{\Delta t}(x_k),$ 
where \(F_{\Delta t}\) denotes one benchmark observation step and \(\Delta t\) is the system-specific observation interval. The forecasting problem is to predict future observation steps at the stored sampling cadence.

The systems of interest are nonlinear and often multi-attractor or multistable. A \emph{fixed point} is a state \(x^\ast\) that remains unchanged under the dynamics, i.e. $F_{\Delta t}(x^\ast) = x^\ast,$ or, in continuous-time notation, \(f(x^\ast)=0\). More generally, the long-run behavior of a trajectory may converge not only to a fixed point but also to a
  more general \emph{attractor} \(A\), such as a limit cycle, torus, or chaotic invariant set. The \emph{basin of attraction} of \(A\) is the set of initial
  conditions whose trajectories converge to \(A\):
  \begin{equation}
      \mathcal{B}(A)
      =
      \left\{
          x_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_x}
          :
          \operatorname{dist}\!\left(F_{\Delta t}^{\,k}(x_0), A\right) \to 0
          \text{ as } k \to \infty
      \right\}.
  \end{equation}

When multiple attractors coexist, the state space is partitioned into multiple basins of attraction. In this regime, different initial conditions can settle into different long-run dynamical modes, and different regions of state space can admit different locally valid linear approximations. 

We learn an encoder $\phi : \mathbb{R}^{d_x} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{d_z}$, a decoder $\psi : \mathbb{R}^{d_z} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{d_x}$, and a latent linear transition matrix $K \in \mathbb{R}^{d_z \times d_z}$, where $d_z$ is the latent dimension. For a batch of $B$ training windows of rollout length $L$,
$\{x_{b,k}, x_{b,k+1}, \dots, x_{b,k+L}\}_{b=1}^{B},$
where $b$ indexes training windows and $\ell$ will denote offsets within a window,
\todo{Fit these three equations into just two lines.}
we encode every state once,
\begin{equation}
    z_{b,k+\ell}^{\mathrm{enc}} = \phi(x_{b,k+\ell}), \qquad \ell = 0, \dots, L,
\end{equation}
roll out the latent transition from the initial encoded state,
\begin{equation}
    z_{b,k+\ell}^{\mathrm{roll}} = K^\ell z_{b,k}^{\mathrm{enc}}, \qquad \ell = 1, \dots, L,
\end{equation}
and decode both the rollout and the encoded future latents,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{x}_{b,k+\ell} = \psi(z_{b,k+\ell}^{\mathrm{roll}}), \qquad
    x_{b,k+\ell}^{\mathrm{rec}} = \psi(z_{b,k+\ell}^{\mathrm{enc}}).
\end{equation}

The cached trajectories are treated as uniformly spaced observation sequences for each system, and the forecasting task is to learn one-step evolution between adjacent stored states rather than the hidden internal steps of the numerical integrator. Accordingly, a horizon of $H$ always means $H$ observed benchmark steps. Because the observation interval is system specific, the same horizon does not correspond to identical physical time across all systems. In this report, we evaluate only forecasting accuracy; we do not assume access to basin labels, basin counts, or any other regime annotations.

% We represent the decoder as a linear dictionary $D \in \mathbb{R}^{d_x \times d_z}$, so that $\hat{x} = D z$. The encoder $\phi(x)$ is implemented as a LISTA network (Alg. \ref{alg:lista}) with parameters $W_e \in \mathbb{R}^{d_z \times d_x}, S \in \mathbb{R}^{d_z \times d_z}$, and thresholds $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^{d_z}_{\ge 0}$; here $W_e$ plays the role of a learned operator analogous to $D^\top$, while $D$ is the dictionary used in reconstruction.

In our implementation, the LISTA model uses the same MLP front end as the MLP baselines to produce a pre-code $c$, then applies one LISTA refinement step:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lista-refinement-step}
u^{(0)} = \operatorname{shrink}(c, \alpha/L),
\qquad
z = \operatorname{ReLU}\!\bigl(\operatorname{shrink}(S u^{(0)} + c, \alpha/L)\bigr).
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{shrink}\!\left(x, \frac{\alpha}{L}\right)
  =
  \operatorname{sign}(x)\,\max\!\left(|x| - \frac{\alpha}{L},\, 0\right)
\end{equation}

Here $S$ is learned, $\alpha$ is the LISTA threshold parameter, and $L$ is a Lipschitz-scale estimate computed from the decoder dictionary. The LISTA model uses a linear dictionary decoder whose atoms are normalized at decode time.

\subsection{Model Families}

We study two encoder classes (MLP and LISTA) and two Koopman transition matrix structures (dense and block diagonal). Combined with a $\ell_1$ sparsity penalty on the Koopman latent $z$, we have five setups, of which two serve as primary benchmarks for our methodological contributions, and three serve as controls.
In the paper-facing benchmark, we fix the latent dimension to $d_z = 256$. For both block-diagonal variants, we use a $256 \times 256$ Koopman matrix composed of sixteen $16 \times 16$ diagonal blocks, with zeros everywhere else and no remainder block.

\paragraph{MLP encoder.} This first model uses an MLP encoder with two hidden layers and bias terms, an linear bias-free decoder, and a dense latent transition matrix $K$. This is the most standard Koopman autoencoder model that serves as a control setup for other training alterations.

\paragraph{MLP encoder + $\ell_1$ penalty on $z$.}
This model family is architecturally identical to the sparse MLP baseline, but includes a small $\ell_1$ penalty on the latent rollout. This serves as a strong architecture-agnostic baseline to elucidate the benefits of sparse latents obtained through regularization, but it does not encode any explicit notion of sparse coding or block-local latent dynamics.

\paragraph{MLP encoder + $\ell_1$ penalty on $z$ + block-diagonal Koopman matrix.}
The third model uses the same MLP encoder and decoder as the dense baseline, but replaces the latent transition with a block-diagonal matrix:
\begin{equation}
    z_{t+1} \approx K_{\mathrm{block}} z_t .
\end{equation}
This setup isolates whether any gains attributed to block-diagonal latent dynamics for multibasin systems can be obtained without the LISTA encoder.

\paragraph{LISTA encoder + $\ell_1$ penalty on $z$.}
The fourth model adds a LISTA refinement step \eqref{eq:lista-refinement-step} to the MLP encoder with $\ell_1$ penalty on $z$. The decoder is a linear dictionary $D \in \mathbb{R}^{d_x \times d_z}$ so that $\psi(z) = Dz$, while the latent dynamics remain fully dense:
\begin{equation}
    z_{t+1} \approx K_{\mathrm{dense}} z_t.
\end{equation}
This comparison isolates the effect of an explicit sparse-coding encoder while keeping the latent dynamics unstructured.

\paragraph{LISTA encoder + $\ell_1$ penalty on $z$ + block-diagonal Koopman matrix.}
The fifth model uses the same LISTA encoder and dictionary decoder, but constrains the latent transition to be block diagonal,
\begin{equation}
    K_{\mathrm{block}}
    =
    \operatorname{blkdiag}\!\big(K^{(1)}, \dots, K^{(M)}\big).
\end{equation}
These blocks are model partitions, not ground-truth basins. 
We do \emph{not} assume that the true basin count is known, that one basin corresponds to one block, or that basin identity is available during training. The purpose of the constraint is purely inductive: it suppresses long-range latent coupling and tests whether more local latent dynamics are advantageous on systems with strongly heterogeneous local structure.

\subsection{Periodic Re-Encoding at Test Time}

At test time, we use periodic re-encoding, following the procedure of \citet{fathi_course_2023}. Starting from an encoded state $z_k = \phi(x_k)$ and a re-encoding period $m$ measured in observation steps, we roll the model forward for $m$ latent steps, forecasting
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{z}_{k+m} = K^m z_k, \qquad
    \tilde{x}_{k+m} = \psi(\tilde{z}_{k+m}), \qquad
    z_{k+m} = \phi(\tilde{x}_{k+m}),
\end{equation}
and then repeat this procedure over the rollout horizon. Periodic re-encoding is key to our evaluation because it gives the model a mechanism for correcting accumulated latent drift during long forecasts, effectively refreshing the Koopman representation of the current state.



\subsection{Training Objective}
\label{sec:training-objective}

For a batch of $B$ windows and a training horizon $L$, we optimize prediction, reconstruction, latent alignment, and sparsity terms:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:loss-terms-state}
\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{pred}} =
\frac{1}{BL}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}
\frac{\|\hat{x}_{b,k+\ell} - x_{b,k+\ell}\|_2}{\sqrt{d_x}},
\qquad
\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{rec}} =
\frac{1}{BL}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}
\frac{\|x_{b,k+\ell}^{\mathrm{rec}} - x_{b,k+\ell}\|_2}{\sqrt{d_x}},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:loss-terms-latent}
\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{align}} =
\frac{1}{BL}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}
\|z_{b,k+\ell}^{\mathrm{roll}} - z_{b,k+\ell}^{\mathrm{enc}}\|_2,
\qquad
\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{sp}} =
\frac{1}{BL}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}
\|z_{b,k+\ell}^{\mathrm{roll}}\|_1.
\end{equation}
The total loss is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}
    =
    \frac{1}{L}
    \left(
    \lambda_{\mathrm{pred}} \bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{pred}}
    +
    \lambda_{\mathrm{rec}} \bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{rec}}
    +
    \lambda_{\mathrm{align}} \bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{align}}
    +
    \lambda_{\mathrm{sp}} \bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{sp}}
    \right).
\end{equation}
Here $\lambda_{\mathrm{pred}}$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{rec}}$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{align}}$, and $\lambda_{\mathrm{sp}}$ are nonnegative scalar loss weights.

The observation-space terms are normalized by $\sqrt{d_x}$ so that the same loss weights transfer across the 3-dimensional and 6-dimensional systems in the benchmark. 
\textbf{The latent-space terms are left in their native scale because the same latent dimension is used for all systems, so scale is absorbed by the loss coefficients. }
The prediction and alignment terms encourage latent linearity, the reconstruction term keeps the learned representation close to the data manifold, and the sparsity term encourages selective latent activations.

Forecasting alone does not establish that the latent representation captures regime structure. On benchmark systems with available basin labels, we therefore ask whether basin structure can be recovered from latent trajectories without using labels during training. For each checkpoint we generate $256$ trajectories, encode every timestep, and construct six trajectory-level feature views: modal support, majority support, last-step support, last-step cosine features, trajectory-mean support, and trajectory-mean cosine features. For continuous feature views, we reduce dimensionality with PCA to at most $20$ dimensions before clustering.

Clustering uses $k$-means, with the true number of basins supplied only at evaluation time. The primary score is Adjusted Rand Index (ARI). To measure support reuse directly, we also report the support-separation ratio
\begin{equation}
    r_{\mathrm{ham}}
    =
    \frac{\mathbb{E}[d_H(s_i,s_j) \mid b_i \neq b_j]}
         {\mathbb{E}[d_H(s_i,s_j) \mid b_i = b_j]} .
\end{equation}
Values greater than $1$ indicate that trajectories from the same basin are closer in support space than trajectories from different basins. We prefer this ratio to simpler uniqueness counts because the latter saturate empirically and are not sufficiently discriminative.

In our experiments, we train 10 seeds of a model for each system and report the interquartile mean (IQM) over these 10 seeds to reduce sensitivity to unstable seeds. We report the mean squared error (MSE) in forecasting predictions from an initial condition.

All models were trained under matched budgets (10 seeds/system, fixed latent dimension, fixed optimization steps, shared checkpointing protocol); full details of the systems, data generation, hyperparameter selection, training and evaluation protocol are in Appendix~\ref{appendix:experimental-protocol}.

\subsection{Fixed-17 Basin-Support Interpretability Study}
\label{sec:fixed17-interpretability}

The forecasting results above establish that sparse Koopman autoencoders can
be accurate long-horizon predictors, but forecasting alone does not tell us
whether the latent representation has learned a meaningful regime structure.
Our interpretability study asks a complementary question: when the underlying
system has multiple basins of attraction, does the sparse support of the latent
code behave like an unlabeled basin indicator or local chart selector? The
paper-side causal question is slightly broader than LISTA versus a single
baseline: is some induced sparsity required for a finite-dimensional Koopman
latent to carry several local linearizations at once without destructive
interference? Classical sparse coding and LISTA produce piecewise-affine
encoders whose active set partitions input space into a union of local
subspaces. In a multistable dynamical system, that inductive bias is
attractive because different basins generally admit different local
linearizations, yet finite-dimensional Koopman theory does not support one
globally exact linear chart spanning all basins simultaneously. Sparse support
is therefore a plausible mechanism for stitching together local Koopman
charts, but that hypothesis must be checked empirically rather than assumed.

We evaluate this question on a fixed shortlist of $17$ deterministic
two-dimensional transition-rich systems. The shortlist contains three native
systems designed for explicit chart-switching stress tests
(\texttt{multiwell\_strong\_transition}, \texttt{gated\_local\_linear}, and
\texttt{gated\_transfer\_linear}) together with $14$ calibrated catalog
systems (\texttt{arrested\_spiral}, \texttt{cal\_asymmetric\_3},
\texttt{cal\_high\_cross\_3}, \texttt{cal\_hexagon\_6},
\texttt{cal\_octagon\_8}, \texttt{cal\_pentagon\_5},
\texttt{cal\_square\_4}, \texttt{checkerboard\_potential},
\texttt{duffing\_triple\_well}, \texttt{snic\_multi},
\texttt{transition\_routes\_4}, \texttt{var\_depth\_gradient\_4},
\texttt{var\_diamond\_4}, and \texttt{var\_l\_shape\_5}). These systems were
chosen because they provide clean phase portraits, well-defined endpoint
basins, and a useful range of symmetries, asymmetries, and finite-horizon
transition behaviors. Importantly, the shortlist is fixed: we do not keep
changing the benchmark while interpreting the method. During training, the
model does not receive basin counts or basin labels. Those quantities are used
only at evaluation time on the benchmark systems.

We judge success at three levels.

\paragraph{Exact-Support Level.}
An exact support is the literal thresholded active-set bitmask $S(x)$. At this
level, we ask whether a support identifies basin and whether each basin reuses
only a small number of exact supports. For a support label $S$ and benchmark
basin label $B$, we report $H(B \mid S)$, $H(S \mid B)$, normalized mutual
information, and the dominant-support mass
\begin{equation}
    U_{\mathrm{exact}}
    =
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}
    \sum_{b}
    \max_{s} p(S=s \mid B=b).
\end{equation}
Low $H(B \mid S)$ means a support is basin-pure; low $H(S \mid B)$ means a
basin reuses a small set of supports; high $U_{\mathrm{exact}}$ means each
basin has a dominant support template.

\paragraph{Support-Family and Dominant-Group Level.}
Exact support can be too brittle, because small coefficient changes can flip a
few active bits without changing the effective local chart. For that reason,
we also use two coarser notions of support identity. A \emph{support family},
denoted $F(x)$, is a cluster of exact supports that are very similar to each
other. In the current reducer, these families are built by greedy clustering
with a Jaccard-overlap rule on the exact support bitmasks, so two supports
such as $\{1,4,7\}$ and $\{1,4,8\}$ can count as the same family if they
differ only by a small atom swap rather than by a genuinely different sparse
pattern. Thus $H(F \mid B)$ asks whether each basin reuses one small family of
nearby supports even when it does not reuse one literal bitmask.

When the latent coordinates are partitioned into explicit groups or blocks
$G_1,\dots,G_M$, we also define the \emph{dominant latent group}
\begin{equation}
    G(x) = \arg\max_{m} \|z_{G_m}(x)\|_2 .
\end{equation}
This discards the exact within-group bit pattern and asks only which latent
block carries the largest activation mass. Dominant-group alignment is
therefore an even coarser success criterion than support-family alignment:
exact support is the strongest notion, support family is the next fallback,
and dominant latent group is the coarsest fallback. This hierarchy matters
because exact support can be brittle. A model may fail to learn one exact
bitmask per basin while still learning one basin-specific support family or
one basin-specific dominant group. In the current phase-$1$ reducer, support
families are based on raw support overlap only; symmetry-aligned family
merging remains future work.

\paragraph{Local-Law Level.}
At the strongest mechanistic reading, support should not merely correlate with
basin; it should select a distinct local linear law. For this we fit
support-conditioned local operators in latent space, compute within-basin
versus between-basin operator distances, measure support persistence and
switch timing along trajectories, and perform intervention tests in which we
project latents onto canonical basin supports or freeze those supports over
multiple rollout steps. These intervention metrics are summarized by ratios
such as own-support over baseline prediction error and freeze-support over
baseline error, where smaller values indicate that the hypothesized support
remains operational after the intervention.

\paragraph{Basis-Aware Interpretation.}
This interpretability protocol is intentionally basis-aware. Two basins can
share similar local spectra while differing mainly in eigendirections,
orientation, or symmetry transform. Accordingly, we do not define success as
``different basin, different eigenvalues.'' Instead, we regard exact-support
reuse as the strongest outcome, support-family or dominant-group reuse as the
next-best outcome, and operator-family or Jacobian-family alignment as a
weaker but still meaningful positive when different basins appear to realize
the same intrinsic local law in different coordinates.

The best-performing LISTA variants on this branch are not the original
one-step nonnegative models. The current frontier is built around sign-split
codes, either with a dense soft-block Koopman matrix or with a block-diagonal
Koopman matrix, together with near-separatrix hard-initialization for the most
promising roots. The dense soft-block sign-split model with latent dimension
$64$ currently gives the strongest exact-support compression, while the
block-diagonal sign-split model is the strongest forecast-retaining regime
structure model. We also explored restrained linear pre-codes and decoder
coherence penalties, and we have implemented but not yet finished evaluating
deeper LISTA refinement, momentum, adaptive and groupwise thresholds,
group-aware shrinkage, top-$k$ group-first selection, dictionary-tied or
hybrid pre-codes, and richer event-triggered reset rules. The model recipe is
therefore still a work in progress: the shortlist leaders are clear enough to
interpret, but the final paper recipe is not yet frozen.

\subsection{Current Interpretability Results on the Fixed-17 Test Bed}
\label{sec:fixed17-interpretability-results}

The completed evidence so far supports a consistent but nuanced picture.
First, basin purity is easier to achieve than exact-support reuse. On the
historical native-trio smoke reduction used to validate the new state-level
metrics, deep-basin $H(B \mid S)$ is already approximately zero for both dense
and block-diagonal LISTA across several support definitions, while
$H(S \mid B)$ remains materially larger on thresholded supports. In other
words, current supports can already tell us which basin a state belongs to
without yet collapsing to one canonical support per basin. This distinction is
central to the study: support-basin correlation is not sufficient evidence for
interpretable chart reuse.

Second, the first strong exact-support positive emerges only after the
sign-split and latent-structure sweeps. On the fixed-$17$ shortlist, the best
exact-support root from the completed \texttt{v5} packet is the dense
soft-block sign-split model with latent dimension $64$, which achieves deep
$H(S \mid B)=0.7719$, $U_{\mathrm{exact}}=0.8064$, and
$H(F \mid B)=0.0521$ while still keeping $16/17$ systems below the working
$H1000$ good-forecast gate. By contrast, the best forecast-retaining root in
that same packet is the block-diagonal sign-split model, which reaches a
system-median best-periodic $H1000$ error of $0.0119$ with $17/17$ good
systems, but with weaker exact-support compression. This establishes a real
frontier rather than a single winner: denser soft-block dynamics help exact
support concentration, whereas harder block structure helps long-horizon
forecast stability.

Third, the subsequent identifiability-oriented modifications help only
partially. Decoder coherence improves forecasting for some dense soft-block
models, whereas restrained linear pre-codes reduce support fragmentation in
some cases, but the combined \texttt{v6} follow-up does not displace the
\texttt{v5} leaders on the overall forecast-versus-interpretability tradeoff.
Similarly, near-separatrix hard-initialization helps the two shortlist leaders
in different ways: it is a clearer interpretability positive for the
block-diagonal root, but a clearer forecasting and intervention positive for
the dense soft-block $p=64$ root. These differing behaviors support the view
that the remaining problem is not one-dimensional. Some models fail because
their supports are too fragmented, while others fail because their supports are
not stable enough to remain operative under intervention.

Table~\ref{tab:fixed17-interpretability-results} summarizes the current
paper-facing comparison on the common deep-basin slice at

useful local chart. The locked packet's fourth arm should be read as a

tanh no-shrink anti-sparsity baseline, but it is still scientifically useful
because it isolates the effect of removing the explicit penalty while keeping a
weaker architectural form of induced sparsity.

Taken together, these results support a claim that is stronger than simple
basin purity but weaker than universal one-support-per-basin identifiability.
LISTA does learn basin-selective and often reusable support structure on the
fixed-$17$ benchmark, and it does so more convincingly than the matched sparse
MLP control when we include intervention-based diagnostics rather than entropy
metrics alone. At the same time, the results do not yet justify the strongest
possible statement that every basin across the shortlist is represented by one
canonical exact support. The most honest current interpretation is that sparse
Koopman supports align reliably at the level of basin selectivity and often at
the level of support families or dominant groups, while exact-support reuse is
present but still conditional on architecture, data sampling near
separatrices, and the particular diagnostic slice. The locked $10$-seed
confirmatory packet now fixes the main paper-facing conclusion more clearly
than the earlier seed-$0$ rerun did. On the selected slice, the dense
soft-block $p=64$ hard-init LISTA root is the strongest basin-support-alignment
result overall: it reaches $H(S \mid B)=0.0543$, $U_{\mathrm{exact}}=0.9923$,
and a freeze/base score of $0.1691$ while keeping all $17/17$ systems below the
working $H1000$ forecast gate. Against the matched sparse MLP control it wins
$H(S \mid B)$ on $15/17$ systems, $U_{\mathrm{exact}}$ on $14/17$,
freeze/base on $16/17$, and persistence on $17/17$. This is the
cleanest evidence on this branch that basin-support alignment can approach a
one-support-per-basin picture on a benchmark system family without using basin
labels during training.

The block-diagonal hard-init LISTA root remains important, but for a different
reason. It is the best forecast-retaining root in the locked packet at
$H1000=0.0516$, and it also keeps a strong freeze/base score of
$0.2665$, which makes it the better companion result for the claim that sparse
supports can remain operational under long periodic rollouts. What it does not
do is beat the dense soft-block hard-init root on exact-support fragmentation.
The paper should therefore treat block-diagonal hard-init as the
forecast-stable companion, not as the lead exact-support result.

The MLP controls clarify what the branch objective is really testing. The
matched sparse MLP control shows that LISTA is not the only route to a useful
sparse latent, but it is still materially worse on exact-support reuse and
support freezing damage than the dense hard-init LISTA root. The locked
packet's zero-$\ell_1$ ReLU control is scientifically meaningful in the same
direction: its $H(S \mid B)=0.1530$ and $U_{\mathrm{exact}}=0.9781$ show that
entropy-style support metrics can still look superficially good even when the
explicit sparsity penalty is removed, while its worse forecasting and
freeze/base score of $0.7162$ show that these supports are much less
persistent and much less useful as basin-local charts. This is exactly why the
packet should not be discarded just because it is not the clean no-shrink
baseline: it tells us that ReLU-induced architectural shrinkage alone may still
matter. A smaller clean tanh no-shrink screen is already much worse in
forecasting (\(H100/H500/H1000 = 1.8317 / 3.5797 / 4.1857\) at the working
budget), so the remaining decisive control is the locked-budget tanh no-shrink
rerun on the same fixed-$17$ systems.

The paper-facing claim should therefore not be that LISTA alone is special. It
should be that some induced sparsity is essential for learning good
finite-dimensional Koopman representations in systems with multiple basins or
fixed points, and that LISTA is one structured way to realize that sparsity.
On the current evidence, the dense soft-block hard-init LISTA root is the
strongest exact-support realization of that idea, the block-diagonal hard-init
root is the strongest forecast-retaining companion, and the clean tanh
no-shrink rerun is the experiment that can either confirm or weaken the
broader induced-sparsity narrative.

\subsection{Forecasting by Basin Depth and Separatrix Proximity}
\label{sec:fixed17-depth-forecast}

The missing half of the mechanism story is state-conditioned forecasting. The
support-side metrics already agree with the intended narrative: supports are
cleaner deep inside a basin and more fragmented near basin boundaries. The
next sub-study should ask whether forecasting follows the same pattern. The
benchmark-only prediction is simple: forecasting should be best when the model
is deep inside a basin and can identify one local linearization confidently,
and worst when the initial state is close to a separatrix where basin identity
and chart assignment are ambiguous.

The protocol should use the same fixed $17$ systems and the same frozen model
set: block-diagonal hard-init LISTA, dense soft-block $p=64$ hard-init LISTA,
the matched sparse MLP control, and the clean tanh no-shrink MLP control once
that rerun completes. Basin labels and basin-depth scores are used only at
evaluation time. For each held-out forecast start, assign a benchmark-only
depth score or depth slice (for example boundary / middle / deep, or the
existing \texttt{boundary} versus \texttt{deep} summaries at
\texttt{absolute:0.001}), balance sample counts within basin and system, and
then report forecasting errors at $H100$, $H500$, and $H1000$. The primary
read should use the official best-periodic checkpoint/cadence rule already used
elsewhere in the benchmark so that the comparison stays aligned with the main
forecast packet; a secondary robustness check can fix one shared cadence to
show that the depth trend is not an artifact of cadence tuning alone.

The main statistics should be paired boundary-versus-deep comparisons within
each system and seed: boundary-minus-deep error gaps, boundary/deep error
ratios, and system-level sign tests across the fixed benchmark. These forecast
numbers should be reported alongside support ambiguity diagnostics such as
support entropy, support-family purity, or support-switch rate, because the
mechanistic story is that near-separatrix states are exactly where support
identification becomes least reliable. If the clean tanh no-shrink control is
uniformly worse and also fails to exploit deep-basin states as effectively,
then the paper can honestly say that induced sparsity is essential for good
finite-dimensional Koopman representations in multi-basin systems, with LISTA
as one particularly structured way to induce that sparsity.
