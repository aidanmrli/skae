\section{What the study is trying to show}

The core objective of the basin-support interpretability study is not merely to
show that a Koopman autoencoder forecasts well on multistable systems. We
already have substantial forecasting evidence. The scientific question here is
stronger:
\begin{quote}
Can a sparse Koopman representation learned without basin labels organize the
state space into latent supports that align with basins of attraction or with
basin-specific local dynamical laws?
\end{quote}

In the intended training and deployment setting, we do \emph{not} assume that
the number of basins is known, and we do \emph{not} assume that trajectories
come with basin labels. Basin information is therefore not a training signal.
It is only an evaluation-time diagnostic on benchmark systems. This matters
because it changes the meaning of success: we are not testing whether a
supervised classifier can recover known basin labels; we are testing whether
the geometry and dynamics themselves are strong enough for sparse supports to
emerge as reusable latent regime indicators.

The study is therefore about three nested questions.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item Do sparse supports identify basin membership?
    \item Do those supports identify distinct local linear laws?
    \item When basin transitions occur in transition-rich benchmarks, do
    support changes happen at the right times and for the right reasons?
\end{enumerate}

The strongest possible outcome would be one canonical sparse support per basin.
But that is intentionally treated as the strongest outcome, not the only honest
one. The live study plan already recognizes a hierarchy of success:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item exact-support reuse,
    \item support-family or dominant-group alignment,
    \item symmetry-aware operator-family or Jacobian-family alignment.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The Koopman-theoretic phenomenon we are studying}

Consider a discrete-time nonlinear dynamical system
\begin{equation}
    x_{t+1} = f(x_t), \qquad x_t \in \mathcal{X} \subset \R^{d_x}.
\end{equation}
Koopman theory asks for observables $\phi(x)$ such that the dynamics are
approximately linear in the lifted coordinates
\begin{equation}
    z_t = \phi(x_t), \qquad z_{t+1} \approx K z_t.
\end{equation}

For a system with a single attractor and favorable structure, one can hope for
a finite-dimensional lifting that behaves nearly globally. In a multistable
system with multiple basins of attraction, that global picture breaks down.
Different basins admit different local linear descriptions, and one finite
continuous chart generally cannot represent all of them exactly while still
containing the original state coordinates in a finite-dimensional
Koopman-invariant subspace.

That is why the model class in this repo is not just a linear latent predictor.
It is a \emph{sparse} Koopman autoencoder with periodic or event-triggered
reencoding:
\begin{equation}
    z_t = \Enc(x_t), \qquad \tilde z_{t+1} = K z_t, \qquad \hat x_{t+1} = \Dec(\tilde z_{t+1}),
\end{equation}
followed, when we reset, by
\begin{equation}
    z_{t+1} = \Enc(\hat x_{t+1}).
\end{equation}
When the encoder is LISTA-like and sparse, this architecture has a natural
interpretability hypothesis:
\begin{quote}
the active support of $z_t$ may function as an implicit chart label, so that
different supports correspond to different locally valid linear regimes.
\end{quote}

The key point is that sparse coding turns the encoder into a piecewise-affine
map. The support pattern
\begin{equation}
    S_t = \Supp(z_t)
\end{equation}
partitions state space into regions where the same small subset of atoms is
active. If the Koopman model is behaving the way we hope, those support regions
should line up with basin geometry, or at least with basin-specific local
dynamical laws.

\section{Why basin eigenstructure matters}

The basin story is not just a clustering story. It is fundamentally a
\emph{local linearization} story. Let $x_b^\star$ be an attracting fixed point
for basin $b$. Near that fixed point,
\begin{equation}
    x_{t+1} - x_b^\star \approx J_b (x_t - x_b^\star),
\end{equation}
where $J_b$ is the Jacobian of the true dynamics near the attractor. A
Koopman-style local chart around basin $b$ is useful if it captures the local
eigenstructure of this linearization: contraction or expansion rates, stable
subspaces, and the directions in which perturbations relax.

However, the study should \emph{not} define success as ``different basins must
have different eigenvalues.'' Two different basins can be dynamically similar
in a deeper sense:
\begin{equation}
    J_{b_2} \approx Q^{-1} J_{b_1} Q
\end{equation}
for some rotation, reflection, signed permutation, or other simple change of
basis. In that case the two basins may share nearly identical local rates while
differing mainly in orientation, symmetry transform, or encoder chart.

This is exactly why the live plan insists on a basis-aware and
symmetry-aware interpretation:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item Two basins may have very similar spectra but different eigenvectors.
    \item Two basins may be symmetry-related rather than dynamically unrelated.
    \item Exact support uniqueness may therefore be too strict even when the
    model has learned the right local law family.
\end{enumerate}

This implies different levels of attainable support success.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item If two basins truly require distinct local coordinates, the ideal
    outcome is one sparse support template per basin.
    \item If two basins share the same intrinsic law up to alignment, the model
    may learn different exact supports but one support family, or one operator
    family after alignment.
    \item If the model only captures coarse basin identity and not a canonical
    sparse chart, dominant-group alignment may still be the honest result.
\end{itemize}

\section{How the study judges success}

The current metric stack is built to separate basin purity, support
compression, dynamical usefulness, and switch behavior. That separation is
important because a representation can succeed on one of these axes while
failing on another.

\subsection{State subsets}

Metrics are not interpreted uniformly over state space. The current study
stratifies states into at least three subsets:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{deep-basin states}: far from separatrices, where a basin code
    should be most stable,
    \item \textbf{boundary states}: near ambiguous regions, where support
    fragmentation is expected,
    \item \textbf{transition-rich trajectories}: where support switching can be
    compared against basin switching.
\end{itemize}

This stratification is essential. A model that only looks clean deep inside a
basin but chatters near boundaries may still be useful, but that is a weaker
claim than ``one support per basin everywhere.''

\subsection{Exact-support metrics}

Given a support rule such as an absolute threshold, relative threshold, or
top-$k$ active set, define a support label $S$ for each state. The main exact
support metrics are:
\begin{align}
    H(B \mid S), \qquad H(S \mid B), \qquad \mathrm{NMI}(S,B),
\end{align}
and
\begin{equation}
    U_{\text{exact}}
    =
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}
    \sum_{b}
    \max_s p(S=s \mid B=b).
\end{equation}

Their roles are different.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item Low $H(B \mid S)$ means a support points mostly to one basin. This is
    \emph{basin purity}.
    \item Low $H(S \mid B)$ means one basin reuses only a small number of exact
    supports. This is \emph{within-basin support compression}.
    \item High $U_{\text{exact}}$ means each basin has one dominant exact
    support.
\end{itemize}

The current branch has already made a critical distinction here: low
$H(B \mid S)$ alone is not enough. A model can be basin-pure while still using
many exact supports per basin. That is why the active paper question is no
longer ``does support correlate with basin at all?'' but rather ``does each
basin reuse a sparse support in a stable way?''

\subsection{Support families and dominant groups}

Exact support is brittle. Small coefficient changes can flip a few active bits
without changing the actual local law. For that reason the reducer also builds
support families by clustering exact supports with a Jaccard-overlap rule, and
it reports the family analogues of the entropy and purity metrics, especially
$H(F \mid B)$.

When the latent is structurally partitioned into groups or blocks, the reducer
also reports dominant-group labels
\begin{equation}
    G(x) = \arg\max_m \norm{z_{G_m}}_2.
\end{equation}

This matters because the study's real objective is \emph{basin-support
alignment}, not literal one-bitmask-per-basin perfection. If exact supports are
too fine-grained but support families or dominant groups line up well with
basins, that is still a meaningful interpretability positive.

\subsection{Trajectory stability and switch metrics}

Support should not only be basin-selective; it should also be temporally
stable. The reducer therefore measures:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item support persistence inside a basin,
    \item support-switch rate on true basin switches,
    \item support-switch rate off true basin switches,
    \item switch detection fraction, miss fraction, delay, false early
    switches, chatter, and dwell times.
\end{itemize}

These metrics are important because a code can look sparse and basin-pure in
aggregate while still switching constantly along a single trajectory. If that
happens, support is not serving as a stable local chart.

\subsection{Operator-family and Jacobian-family metrics}

The support story only becomes dynamical if the supports select different local
linear laws. For each support class $c$, the reducer fits a support-conditioned
local operator
\begin{equation}
    A_c
    =
    \argmin_A
    \sum_{t:\,c_t=c}
    \norm{z_{t+1} - A z_t}_2^2
    + \lambda \norm{A}_F^2,
\end{equation}
and it compares these with basin-conditioned operators $A_b$ fit from the true
benchmark basin labels.

The main raw summaries are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item support-vs-basin operator distance,
    \item within-basin operator distance,
    \item between-basin operator distance,
    \item the ratio
    $\text{between}/\text{within}$.
\end{itemize}

The same logic is applied in state space using effective Jacobians of the
learned one-step predictor
\begin{equation}
    \hat f(x) = \Dec(K \Enc(x)),
    \qquad
    J_{\hat f}(x) = \frac{\partial \hat f}{\partial x}(x).
\end{equation}

The current implementation already supports the \emph{raw} operator-family and
Jacobian-family distances. The next planned refinement is basis-aware
alignment: compare not only $\norm{A_b - A_{b'}}_F$ but also
\begin{equation}
    \min_{Q \in \mathcal{Q}} \norm{A_{b'} - Q^{-1} A_b Q}_F,
\end{equation}
with $\mathcal{Q}$ restricted to interpretable alignments such as orthogonal or
signed-permutation transforms. This is the right way to handle basins that
share spectrum but differ in eigendirection orientation.

\subsection{Interventional metrics}

The study also includes direct interventions on support, because correlation is
not enough.

\paragraph{Canonical support projection.}
For each basin, define its canonical support template from deep-basin states.
Then compare one-step prediction error under:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item the model's native latent,
    \item the same latent projected onto its own basin's canonical support,
    \item the same latent projected onto another basin's canonical support.
\end{enumerate}

If the support is genuinely causal for the local chart, then own-basin
projection should hurt little relative to baseline, while wrong-basin
projection should hurt badly.

\paragraph{Freeze-support rollouts.}
The reducer also freezes a basin's canonical support mask over longer rollout
horizons. This tests whether the learned local law really operates through a
stable support over time rather than merely through a transient one-step mask.

\section{What the current evidence already suggests}

The completed documents now support a more specific and more stable
interpretation.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item Deep inside basin, basin purity can already be very strong. On the
    native smoke subset, $H(B \mid S)$ is already approximately zero for both
    dense and block-diagonal LISTA under some support definitions.
    \item Exact-support reuse is harder. Away from the selected deep-basin
    slice, $H(S \mid B)$ remains materially larger unless support is forced
    into aggressive top-$k$ masks.
    \item The family-level story is still cleaner than the exact-support story
    in aggregate. Support-family entropy within basin is often much lower than
    exact-support entropy even when exact canonical support reuse is imperfect.
    \item The strongest completed exact-support result is now the locked dense
    soft-block sign-split $p=64$ hard-init root from the $200$k, $10$-seed
    confirmatory packet. On the selected deep-basin slice it reaches
    $H(S \mid B)=0.0543$, $U_{\text{exact}}=0.9923$,
    \texttt{freeze/base@20}$=0.1691$, and $H1000=0.0768$.
    \item The same locked packet keeps the block-diagonal hard-init root as
    the forecast-retaining companion ($H1000=0.0516$,
    \texttt{freeze/base@20}$=0.2665$), but not as the lead exact-support
    result.
    \item The narrow default-sampling $200$k follow-up on the best
    \texttt{v6}/\texttt{v7} forecasting roots is negative for promotion: the
    better coherence root reaches
    $H100/H500/H1000 = 0.0416 / 0.0761 / 0.0796$, which still does not beat
    the locked hard-init finalists and adds no new state-level reduction.
\end{itemize}

The honest current paper read is therefore no longer ``the shortlist is still
moving.'' Training-side ranking is effectively closed. The defensible claim is
that LISTA can produce basin-selective and, on the selected deep-basin slice,
very strong exact-support structure under the promoted hard-init dense root,
but the branch as a whole still needs claim calibration between exact-support
reuse and family/group-level alignment because the strongest causal evidence is
mixed-regime and the remaining evaluation-side diagnostics are not done.

\section{How the model is being changed to encourage sparse, reusable supports}

The method-side work is not random hyperparameter search. Each change is meant
to bias the model toward a support representation that is sparse, reusable, and
dynamically meaningful.

\subsection{The Koopman transition structure}

The study compares three main $K$ families:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{dense $K$}: maximally flexible, weakest built-in
    interpretability,
    \item \textbf{hard block-diagonal $K$}: strongest structural bias toward
    basin-specific subspaces,
    \item \textbf{soft block-sparse $K$}: a compromise that allows mostly local
    dynamics with limited cross-block sharing.
\end{itemize}

This matters because the encoder and the linear dynamics must cooperate. If the
latent support is basin-structured but $K$ freely mixes every coordinate, the
support semantics become less trustworthy.

\subsection{Dynamics-aware reencoding}

The most important encoder-side change is that reencoding is no longer treated
as a fresh solve from scratch. The codebase now supports encoding with a latent
warm start:
\begin{equation}
    z_t = \Enc(x_t; \tilde z_t),
\end{equation}
where $\tilde z_t$ is the predicted latent before reset. In practice this means
the encoder can use the current forecast as a dynamics prior. Conceptually,
this turns reencoding into a proximal correction around the current chart,
rather than a complete chart re-selection from nothing.

\subsection{Signed, nonnegative, and sign-split codes}

The repo explicitly studies whether the final LISTA code should be:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item nonnegative,
    \item signed,
    \item or sign-split.
\end{itemize}

The sign-split variant maps a signed pre-activation $v$ to
\begin{equation}
    z = \bigl[\max(v,0), \max(-v,0)\bigr].
\end{equation}
This keeps support semantics clean while preserving sign information. In the
current shortlist documents, sign-split is one of the main positive method-side
changes behind the strongest exact-support results.

\subsection{More LISTA refinement and momentum}

One LISTA step can be too shallow near separatrices or after long rollouts. The
repo now exposes:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item deeper LISTA refinement loops,
    \item fixed-beta momentum between refinement steps,
    \item HyperLISTA-style adaptive refinement in separate branches.
\end{itemize}

The intended effect is not only better reconstruction; it is cleaner
convergence to a sparse support that is stable under small perturbations.

\subsection{Adaptive and groupwise thresholds}

A single global shrinkage threshold is unlikely to be correct everywhere in
phase space. The current LISTA stack therefore supports:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item sample-dependent thresholds driven by reconstruction residual and
    latent-prior mismatch,
    \item groupwise learned base thresholds over inferred latent groups.
\end{itemize}

This is important because different parts of state space can demand different
amounts of sparsification. Deep in a basin, a sharper threshold may stabilize a
canonical support; near a boundary, a softer threshold may prevent brittle
premature switching.

\subsection{Group-aware shrinkage and top-$k$ group selection}

If the basin hypothesis is really ``one dominant sparse support family per
basin,'' then pure coordinatewise shrinkage is not ideal. The codebase now also
supports:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item sparse-group shrinkage over inferred latent groups,
    \item top-$k$ group-first masking before within-group thresholding.
\end{itemize}

These changes are meant to encourage a two-level structure:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item a basin-level or regime-level group turns on,
    \item sparse coordinates within that group encode local variation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Pre-code control: free, linear, dictionary-tied, hybrid}

The pre-threshold LISTA input $c$ strongly affects identifiability. If the
pre-code MLP is too expressive, it can bypass the sparse-coding bias entirely.
The repo therefore exposes:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \texttt{free\_mlp},
    \item \texttt{linear},
    \item \texttt{dictionary\_tied},
    \item \texttt{hybrid}.
\end{itemize}

The dictionary-tied and hybrid modes are especially relevant for
interpretability because they keep the encoder anchored to the live decoder
dictionary instead of letting the pre-code become an opaque free feature map.

\subsection{Decoder coherence and support identifiability}

The docs also emphasize decoder coherence penalties. If dictionary atoms are
too redundant, many different exact supports can represent nearly the same
state. Reducing atom coherence should therefore reduce fake support
multiplicity and make exact-support interpretation more credible.

\subsection{Event-triggered resets and trigger scores}

The evaluation stack now supports event-triggered reencoding using label-free
scores such as:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item projection gap,
    \item group ambiguity,
    \item off-block spillover,
    \item support-margin fragility,
    \item minimum dwell and maximum interval constraints.
\end{itemize}

These are not only rollout tricks. They are also mechanistic probes. A good
trigger should fire when the model is leaving a local chart or becoming
support-ambiguous, not constantly inside an easy basin.

\subsection{Important implementation caveat}

The current implementation does not expose every design axis in every possible
combination. In particular, the sign-split branch is currently separated from
some adaptive-threshold, groupwise-threshold, group-shrinkage, top-$k$ group,
and dictionary-tied/hybrid pre-code branches. So in the present repo, some of
these ideas are parallel ablation families rather than one unified model.

\section{Experiments and Results So Far}

\subsection{Experimental scope}

The completed evidence now comes from four layers: legacy native-trio smoke
reductions, seed-$0$ fixed-shortlist screening on the fixed $17$ systems at
$20$k steps, the seed-$0$ hard-init oversampling ablation, and the locked
$200$k, $10$-seed confirmatory packet against the matched sparse MLP control
plus the zero-$L1$ ReLU ablation. There is also one narrow long-budget
default-sampling follow-up on the best forecasting roots from \texttt{v6} and
\texttt{v7}, but that packet is forecasting-only and therefore functions as a
shortlist triage check rather than as new basin-support evidence.

The main paper-facing state-level evidence is now the locked deep-basin
comparison on the \texttt{absolute:0.001} slice. The earlier clean cross-root
rerun remains scientifically useful because it locked the seed-$0$ shortlist
under one common protocol, but it is no longer the final comparison. One
fairness caveat remains central for coauthor handoff: the promoted LISTA
finalists use hard-init oversampling while the MLP controls in the locked
packet use the standard training sampling regime, so the confirmatory packet is
architecture-plus-sampling evidence rather than architecture-only evidence.

This table needs careful interpretation.

First, the dense soft-block sign-split $p=64$ hard-init root is the strongest
completed exact-support result overall. It has the lowest $H(S \mid B)$, the
highest $U_{\text{exact}}$, the best freeze-support robustness, and it remains
forecast-competitive at $H1000=0.0768$. In the context of the study, this is
the clearest completed evidence that LISTA can move beyond basin purity toward
something closer to reusable exact supports.

Second, the block-diagonal hard-init root is not the exact-support leader, but
it is still scientifically important because it keeps the best long-horizon
forecast error and a strong freeze-support ratio. That is consistent with a
different kind of positive: even when the model uses more than one exact
support inside a basin, the basin block itself may behave like a more stable
long-horizon chart. In other words, this root looks more like a persistent
regime-structure model than an exact-support minimizer.

Third, the controls show why entropy-style separation metrics alone cannot
carry the claim. The matched sparse MLP control is reasonably strong on the
selected slice, and the zero-$L1$ ReLU ablation even looks superficially good
on some support statistics, but both are weaker on freeze-support robustness
and at least one long-horizon forecasting axis. The paper therefore has to
present the locked packet as mixed-regime evidence and keep the missing
matched-sampling MLP control as a fairness caveat rather than pretending that
the architecture effect is fully isolated.

\subsection{Overall interpretation of the completed evidence}

Taken together, the completed experiments support four conclusions.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Basin purity is real and relatively easy.} Current LISTA
    models can already make support predict basin very well deep inside basin.
    The native-trio smoke reductions established that point clearly, and the
    locked selected-slice packet saturates $H(B \mid S)=0.0000$ for all four
    compared roots.
    \item \textbf{Exact-support reuse can be strong on the selected deep slice,
    but it is not yet a branch-wide free win.} The dense hard-init root is a
    genuine exact-support positive on the locked packet, yet boundary and
    all-state fragmentation remain much higher, so the final paper claim still
    needs careful wording.
    \item \textbf{Architecture and sampling affect what kind of success is
    obtained.} Dense soft-block sign-split hard-init LISTA is currently the
    strongest completed arm for exact-support compression, while block-diagonal
    hard-init LISTA is the forecast-retaining companion. The default-sampling
    \texttt{v6}/\texttt{v7} roots do not catch up when promoted to $200$k and
    $10$ seeds.
    \item \textbf{The remaining work is not more LISTA recipe search.} The
    training-side ranking is effectively closed. What remains is
    state-conditioned forecasting, alignment-aware or family-level analysis,
    and fairness calibration around the mixed-regime confirmatory packet.
\end{enumerate}

The most important consequence for the overall objective of the study is that
we do not yet have a fully architecture-isolating proof of one canonical exact
support per basin across the whole shortlist. What we do have is locked
multi-seed evidence that the promoted dense hard-init LISTA root yields much
cleaner exact-support reuse and freeze robustness than the matched sparse MLP
control on the clean deep-basin slice, plus a negative long-budget
default-sampling follow-up showing that standard-sampling \texttt{v6}/\texttt{v7}
roots do not supersede it. The remaining question is therefore not which LISTA
recipe wins, but how aggressively the paper should phrase that win.

\section{What would count as a convincing positive}

The strongest paper-grade positive would look like this.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item Deep-basin $H(B \mid S)$ and $H(S \mid B)$ are both low, with high
    $U_{\text{exact}}$.
    \item Support families or dominant groups remain stable along trajectories
    except near real basin changes.
    \item Own-basin projection and freeze-support interventions preserve
    prediction much better than wrong-basin interventions.
    \item Support-conditioned operator and Jacobian families are tight within
    basin and separated across basins, with alignment-aware comparisons used
    when spectra are similar but eigendirections differ.
    \item The result still holds against the matched MLP control.
\end{enumerate}

But a weaker result can still be scientifically valuable. If exact supports
fragment yet support families, dominant groups, or aligned operator families
line up well with basin structure, then the correct claim is not failure. It is
that sparse Koopman supports identify basin-selective local law families more
reliably than they identify one exact canonical support per basin.

\section{Bottom line}

The basin-support interpretability study is trying to determine whether sparse
supports in a Koopman autoencoder are merely a convenient compression device or
whether they actually function as unlabeled regime indicators for multistable
dynamics. Koopman theory motivates the need for local charts; basin
eigenstructure explains why those charts may differ by more than just
eigenvalues; and the study's metric stack is designed to tell apart exact chart
reuse, family-level reuse, and weaker but still meaningful operator-family
alignment.

The current documents already point to a specific scientific stance: basin
purity is real, exact-support uniqueness is only partially achieved, and the
most defensible near-term claim may land at support-family or dominant-group
alignment, with operator/Jacobian-family analysis used to decide how strong the
final paper claim can honestly be.
